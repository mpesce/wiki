# Voices of VR Mark Pesce 20Nov20.mp3

## Summary

This recording is an interview featuring Mark Pesce, a media and technology commentator and former Apple and Mozilla engineer, discussing the themes and history of his book on augmented reality. The conversation covers the origins of AR in 1950s military jet fighter cockpit design and J.C.R. Licklider’s work at the Information Processing Technologies Office, which led to Ivan Sutherland’s development of the Sword of Damocles head-mounted display in 1968. Pesce argues that AR is not a novel invention but the natural consequence of thirty years of human-computer interaction design, specifically the integration of surveillance techniques into software to track user behavior. He highlights that for AR to function, devices must use simultaneous localization and mapping to constantly watch the physical environment, making it inherently a technology of surveillance. Additionally, he notes that modern AR systems, such as Facebook’s Project Aria, also track user gaze direction, revealing private physiological data about desires and preferences. The discussion traces the commercial development of AR, crediting Microsoft’s HoloLens and Kinect technology as foundational to modern inside-out tracking, rather than Facebook. Pesce explains that Facebook’s aggressive push into AR in 2017 was likely a strategic move to counter Snapchat, but he warns of the dangers of commercial entities shaping AR narratives. He cites the 2016 Pokemon Go incident in Rhodes, Australia, where a small park became a site of massive, unintended social gathering, as a primary example of how AR transforms space and human behavior. This leads to a broader discussion on agency, privacy, and the legal implications of placing metadata in physical space. Pesce proposes the creation of a regulatory body similar to ICANN to manage locative metadata and protect property rights against unauthorized digital overlays. Pesce concludes by framing AR as a critical inflection point comparable to the rise of the web, where the physical world will begin to speak for itself through data. He emphasizes the need for comprehensive policy and social infrastructure to manage the feedback loops between humans, devices, and cloud networks. The interview touches on the concept of animism in technology, referencing Sherry Turkle’s research on children and Furbys to illustrate how interactive systems change our ontological relationship with the world. The speaker expresses hope that AR can provide contextual depth to the built environment, allowing users to understand the interiority of objects and spaces, but stresses that this requires careful navigation to avoid dystopian outcomes where algorithms manipulate human agency.
