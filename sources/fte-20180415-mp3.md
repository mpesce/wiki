## Summary

This recording is an episode of the program Future Tense, hosted by Anthony Fennell, which explores the blurring lines between reality and artificiality in the digital age. The discussion features several experts, including Mark Pesce, an honorary associate in the Digital Cultures program at the University of Sydney, who argues that machine learning systems are now capable of profiling users and steering their perception of the world. Pesce notes that platforms like Facebook curate feeds to maximize engagement rather than reflect objective reality, a trend exacerbated by the rise of augmented reality technologies that overlay digital content onto the physical world. He warns that this creates a personalized fantasy that makes it increasingly difficult to distinguish the real world from a constructed one. The conversation shifts to the specific threat of deepfakes, with Hani Farid, a professor of computer science at Dartmouth College, explaining how deep learning algorithms can superimpose one person’s face onto another’s body in video. Farid describes the technology as a democratization of sophisticated digital fakery, posing serious risks to democracy and elections by allowing the creation of convincing false statements by world leaders. He highlights the asymmetry of authentication, noting that while evidence of tampering proves a video is fake, the absence of such evidence does not prove it is real, leading to a crisis of trust where legitimate content can be dismissed as fabricated. Andrew Potter, author of The Authenticity Hoax, joins to discuss the cultural obsession with authenticity, suggesting that modern nostalgia for a "real" past is a form of status seeking that ultimately leads to disappointment because the idealized past never truly existed. Robert Thompson, an expert in television and popular culture at Syracuse University, offers a more nuanced view on artificiality, arguing that viewers of reality TV understand the contrived nature of the content but still derive insight from the kernel of truth in human behavior. He expresses concern over the erosion of shared factual consensus, such as debates over scientific facts, but notes that the core issue is the need for better media literacy and ethical education to help citizens navigate the overwhelming volume of information. The episode concludes with a call for greater emphasis on critical thinking and moral education in schools to prepare individuals for the challenges of the information age.
