## Summary

This recording is the closing keynote address delivered by Mark Pesce at the Webstock conference in Wellington, New Zealand. Introduced by the host, who notes Pesce’s previous participation in the event, the speaker begins by thanking the organizers and praising the city’s enthusiastic culture. He references a recent panel with national librarians from Singapore, Australia, and New Zealand to highlight the region’s innovative spirit, specifically noting the New Zealand National Librarian’s vision for a national digital sharing system. Pesce also acknowledges his time with the local company SilverStripe, describing their business model of giving away software as a viable 21st-century approach based on the axiom that shared value increases utility. The core of the talk traces the history of the web from Pesce’s personal experience downloading NCSA Mosaic in 1993 to its explosive growth in 1994. He argues that the web’s success was driven by its ability to act as a "love machine" that reflected individual desires, a dynamic that overwhelmed society with its capacity to hoover up human culture. Pesce identifies two recent developments as the bookends of this "golden age": the digitization of books, which he predicts will largely ephemeralize within a decade, and the arrival of the web as an appliance, exemplified by Apple’s iPad. He posits that these shifts complete the translation of human culture into a universally accessible digital form. Pesce then transitions to his vision for the future, arguing that the next technological revolution will involve making implicit human meanings explicit through augmented reality and mobile devices. He illustrates this with three thought experiments: using a device to reassemble a book’s content into a personalized hypertext experience; scanning a package of beef mince to trace its supply chain, carbon footprint, and nutritional value; and using a handheld device to manage personal health data, including drug interactions and genomic information. He concludes by stating that while the semantic web has focused on machine-to-machine communication, the true potential lies in a seductive, human-centric integration of data with the physical world.
