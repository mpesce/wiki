# National Trust - 'Sense in Place'.mp4

## Summary

Mark Pesce delivers a talk to the National Trust, discussing how augmented reality technology is fundamentally altering the way humans understand and interact with physical places. He begins by comparing traditional memorial plaques on buildings to digital metadata, noting that both serve to affix human-readable information to specific spaces. Pesce argues that while cyberspace has accumulated a vast repository of unlocated data over the last three decades, augmented reality offers a new method to situate this information directly within the physical world. He defines the core function of this technology as the ability to place data in space and make it visible, using an example of a Da Vinci painting appearing on a wall via a smartphone. The speaker outlines a hierarchical approach to annotating the world, moving from the city level of Sydney down to specific neighborhoods like The Rocks, streets like Argyll, and individual structures such as the Argyll Stores. He highlights that while historical data for these locations exists in online encyclopedias, it is not currently accessible in situ. Pesce emphasizes that multiple, often competing narratives can be attached to a single location, citing a Guardian article about the final Mardi Gras party in 2020 as an example of a specific story tied to the Argyll Stores. He notes that as augmented reality devices become as common as smartphones within the next decade, these situated stories will contend for public attention. Pesce concludes by addressing the National Trust’s role in this emerging landscape. He warns that the ability to inscribe metadata into physical space is contentious because it influences behavior and identity. Since changing the data in a space changes how people behave in that space, the Trust must carefully consider who is allowed to write into its controlled environments and what stories are portrayed
