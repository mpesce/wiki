# Mark Pesce 20Nov20.wav

## Summary

This recording is an interview with Mark Pesce, author of the recently released book Augmented Reality Unboxing. Recorded in December 2020, the conversation explores the historical, technical, and societal implications of augmented reality. Pesce, who has worked in the field for thirty years and helped create VRML, argues that AR is not a novel invention but the natural consequence of thirty years of human-computer interaction design. He traces the technology’s lineage to the 1950s, citing J.C.R. Licklider and Ivan Sutherland’s work at the IPTO, and highlights the 1968 Fall Joint Computer Conference as a pivotal moment where the foundations of both the web and AR were established. A central theme of the discussion is the inherent surveillance capability of AR. Pesce explains that for AR to function, devices must use SLAM to constantly map the physical environment, making surveillance unavoidable unlike in web browsing. He further notes that modern AR systems, such as Facebook’s Project Aria, also track gaze direction, revealing intimate user preferences. Pesce criticizes the commercial framing of AR, particularly by Facebook, pointing out that the ability to place digital art in space carries the same risk as placing harmful content, a danger exacerbated by the platform’s history with disinformation. He contrasts this with the early days of Pokemon Go, using the "riot" at Peg Patterson Park in Australia as an example of how AR can unexpectedly transform human behavior and social spaces. The interview concludes with a focus on agency, privacy, and governance. Pesce warns that AR will merge the non-located web with physical space, creating conflicts over who has the right to write metadata into shared environments. He proposes the creation of a regulatory body similar to ICANN to manage locative metadata and emphasizes the need for democratic oversight to counter the power of tech giants. He views the coming decade as a critical inflection point where AR spectacles will become mainstream, requiring society to understand the feedback loops between humans, devices, and autonomous systems to preserve individual agency.
