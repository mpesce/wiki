# mark pesce BTB vodcast iphone (1).m4v

## Summary

This recording is a lecture delivered by Mark Pesce to an audience of educators, likely in Australia, discussing the intersection of network technology and pedagogy. Pesce begins by recounting his history with the education sector, noting that his involvement began around 2004 when he was the first panelist to mention Wikipedia to Australian educators. He argues that the web has undergone three major shifts: the initial emergence of the web in the mid-1990s, the post-dot-com era where the web became an interactive space for sharing and connection, and the 2007 release of Facebook’s F8 platform, which amplified social networking by harnessing the innate human desire to connect. Pesce warns that without clear intentions and strong mentoring, these powerful tools can lead to negative outcomes such as harassment and the spread of harmful content, placing a significant burden on educators to act as positive moral forces. The core of the talk focuses on the concept of the "educational field," a pervasive network of resources and social connections that transcends the traditional classroom. Pesce argues that this field provides the necessary infrastructure to realize the unfinished project of constructivism, a learning theory associated with Jean Piaget which posits that children learn best through play and interaction rather than top-down instruction. He contends that while constructivism has historically been too resource-intensive for industrial-era schooling, the network allows for a distributed model of mentorship. By leveraging tools like Wikipedia and emerging social platforms, educators can create a continuous, supportive web of relationships that connects students, parents, and experts. Pesce specifically addresses the Australian context, suggesting that the new national curriculum should not be viewed merely as a standardized document but as the architecture for a national learning network. He proposes using the curriculum to organize shared resources, identify experts, and facilitate connections between schools and communities. He concludes by urging educators to resist inertia and embrace the network as an inseparable part of the educational system. By standing connected, educators can help ensure that every child benefits from a high-quality education regardless of their circumstances, effectively marrying the demands of formal curriculum with the playful, exploratory nature of constructivist learning.
