# Mark Pesce - Digital Depth in Augmented Reality.mp4

## Summary

This recording is a lecture by Mark Pesce, an Honorary Associate in the Digital Cultures Program at the University of Sydney, on the topic of digital depth in augmented reality. The talk traces the historical development of AR, beginning with Norbert Wiener’s 1948 work on cybernetics and the subsequent need for human-computer symbiosis to manage complex machinery like the F-86A jet fighter. Pesce highlights the contributions of J.C.R. Licklider and Ivan Sutherland, noting that Sutherland’s 1968 head-mounted display, known as the Sword of Damocles, is considered the first augmented reality system because it blended computer-generated views with the user’s real-world vision. The lecture identifies three key technological enablers for modern AR: the Microsoft Kinect for depth sensing, John Hanke’s work on metadata-rich maps through Niantic, and the Google Cardboard project which demonstrated that smartphones possessed sufficient processing power for real-time 3D graphics. Pesce argues that augmented reality is not merely a device but a method of binding metadata to physical space, a practice with ancient roots in rock art and modern examples like the 2016 release of Pokemon Go. He cites an incident in Sydney where the game’s placement of a PokeStop in a small park led to an uncontrolled gathering of thousands of players, illustrating how AR alters human behavior in physical spaces. The lecture also discusses the potential of AR to reveal hidden complexity in systems, such as car engines, thereby supporting the right to repair and enhancing user agency. However, Pesce raises significant ethical concerns, particularly regarding the "right to write" in public spaces and the inherent surveillance capabilities of AR systems. He presents a satirical reworking of Facebook’s Project Aria video, titled Project Panoptic, to highlight the risks of continuous egocentric data collection and user profiling. The talk concludes by warning that while AR offers immense potential, society must establish ethical guidelines before these systems are deployed at scale.
