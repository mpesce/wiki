# Mark interviews Bill.wav

## Summary

This recording is a studio talk and interview featuring Mark Pesce and Dr. William Martin, hosted by Chantal, the founder and CEO of the studio. The session serves as a career retrospective for Martin, a perceptual psychologist and audio engineer, covering his work from the early 1980s to the present. Martin begins by discussing his academic background, including a bachelor’s degree in perceptual psychology and a PhD from Northwestern University, where he developed fundamental research on Head-Related Transfer Functions (HRTFs). He explains how these functions allow humans to perceive spatial audio and environmental acoustics, noting that his 1987 paper significantly reduced the computational cost of this technology, leading to commercialization by companies like Roland. The conversation moves through Martin’s professional history, including the founding of AORUS Corporation, his work at UCLA and the Joint Emu Creative Technology Center on the Sound Blaster AWE32, and his collaboration with Intel on the RSX3D API. He also details his involvement with VRML and a project with Thomas Dolby and Paul Allen’s Integral Research, which explored gesture-based sound creation and internet jamming. A significant portion of the talk is dedicated to Martin’s seven-year tenure at the University of Aizu in Japan, where he began working with high-density loudspeaker arrays. He later joined the University of Sydney, where he utilizes a 196-speaker hemisphere to simulate acoustic environments for architectural design and musical performance, emphasizing the importance of reverberation in music. In the final segment, Martin addresses current trends such as data sonification, the potential of "hearables" for noise management, and the challenges of augmented reality. He argues that effective virtual environments require multimodal consistency, including proprioception and vestibular input, to avoid simulator sickness. The discussion concludes with a Q&A session touching on the emotional impact of sound in film, the necessity of off-head transducers for visually impaired users, and the future of spatial audio in improving quality of life through better communication and data interpretation.
