# The Human Use of Human Networks (1).mp3

## Summary

This recording is a lecture delivered by Mark Pesce, a media and technology commentator and researcher at AFTRS, discussing the convergence of entertainment creation and technology utilization. The talk serves as a preview for his upcoming book, Hyper People, which explores three converging areas: peer-to-peer file sharing, social networks, and personal media culture. Pesce argues that these technologies are revolutionizing consumer bandwidth adoption and upending traditional control over content distribution. He illustrates this shift with the "Doctor Who Effect," where a leaked episode of the revived BBC series was widely distributed via BitTorrent, potentially to generate interest after a US channel declined to purchase it. This demonstrates that distribution control has moved from broadcasters to users, creating a direct economic relationship between producers and audiences while eliminating traditional distribution channels. Pesce highlights that Australians lead the world in per capita television downloads via BitTorrent, signaling a move away from pre-programmed network schedules toward unlimited choice. However, he warns that this abundance leads to decision paralysis, as the distinction between professional and amateur content fades. To solve this, he proposes that digital social networks must evolve from static profiles into active, dynamic entities that function as media filters. He suggests integrating social networks with devices like iTunes and TiVo to allow users to share viewing and listening habits, enabling friends to recommend content based on actual usage data rather than just casual conversation. He also envisions using social networks as spam filters by verifying sender credibility against a user's contact list. The core of the lecture focuses on "knowledge amplification," the idea that social networks can augment human intellect by allowing users to query their social graph for understanding and wisdom, not just facts. Pesce references the Dunbar number, suggesting that digital networks can act as electronic prostheses to help humans manage relationships beyond the biological limit of 150 connections. He concludes by advocating for distributed, user-owned social network software to address privacy concerns and calls on network designers to build infrastructure that delivers "human-ness" rather than just data. The session ends with a Q&A where Pesce discusses the role of social credit in innovation, the adoption of social networks by older generations, and the need for new economic models to support content creators in a disintermediated market
