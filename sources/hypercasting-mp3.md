# hypercasting.mp3

## Summary

This recording is a talk titled "Hypercasting: Part One, The Story So Far," delivered by Mark Pesce. The presentation outlines the seismic shifts in media distribution driven by the internet, arguing that the collapse of traditional barriers has fundamentally altered the economics of content creation and consumption. Pesce begins by reflecting on the evolution of the web from the 1994 revolution through the dot-com bubble and its subsequent "death," which he argues led to a rebirth characterized by peer-to-peer networks, feeds, and torrents. He posits that the essence of digital artifacts is their infinite reproducibility, a quality that professional media industries have resisted despite relying on digital distribution for revenue. This resistance is highlighted by the tension between studios and platforms like YouTube, which initially built its audience on user-uploaded clips of professional content. The speaker then analyzes the Google acquisition of YouTube for 1.65 billion dollars, noting the simultaneous licensing deals struck with major music publishers such as Universal, Sony, BMG, and CBS. Pesce argues that these moves signal a realization by big media that they have lost control of distribution and must now monetize the network rather than fight it. He counters the notion that audiences prefer only professional content by citing successful amateur productions like Red vs. Blue and Homestar Runner, as well as the viral success of the Sick Puppies music video, which reached three million viewers through peer-to-peer sharing. He asserts that salience, or the relevance of content to a specific audience, is the primary driver of media success, not production value. In the final section, Pesce introduces the concept of "hypercasting," a future state of media where personal data and social networks are used to filter and recommend content. He compares the current deluge of unrecorded interaction data to the early discovery of petroleum, suggesting that this "data guck" holds immense value for understanding social connections. Hypercasting would leverage this metadata to automate the sharing of relevant media among friends, effectively turning every user into a broadcaster. The talk concludes by predicting that while YouTube may face challenges, the audience's demand for personalized, high-salience content will drive the transition to hypercasting regardless of the specific platform that facilitates it.
