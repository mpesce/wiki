# Terror and Transhumanism.mp3

## Summary

This recording is a lecture delivered by Mark Pesce to a group of Stanford University students, titled "Terror and Transhumanism." The talk serves as a critical deprogramming session following recent visits by transhumanist figures Eric Drexler and John Smart. Pesce, who identifies as a critic of transhumanism, argues that the movement functions less as a philosophy and more as a religious belief system with unquestioned dogmas. He frames his analysis through the lens of Marshall McLuhan’s media theory, positing that human history is defined by the progressive ingression of intelligence into matter. This trend is characterized by humanity’s increasing ability to manipulate physical objects at finer scales, from stone tools to nanotechnology, alongside the development of linguistic communication. Pesce suggests that these trends are converging to create a world where reality becomes linguistically pliable, allowing for the fabrication of specific realities through code and language. In the second part of the lecture, Pesce introduces the concept of the "shadow," drawing on Carl Jung to explore the darker implications of these technological advancements. He argues that the same capabilities allowing for precise material manipulation are enabling the creation of increasingly sophisticated and accessible weapons, including nuclear, biological, and chemical agents. He highlights the inherent human drive for dominance and control, noting that as individual power increases, so does the potential for catastrophic destruction by a single actor or small group. Pesce cites a conference in Los Angeles where government officials discussed using nanotechnology and pharmacology to create "super soldiers" capable of enduring extreme conditions without sleep or emotional response, illustrating the military’s role in driving transhumanist innovation. The final section addresses the ethical and existential questions of the transhuman future. Pesce critiques the "extropian" belief in uploading consciousness to silicon, arguing it is physically impossible and psychologically unsound due to the necessity of embodiment for sanity. He contrasts utopian visions of a networked, hierarchical-free society with dystopian scenarios of totalitarian control, such as the Matrix or Skynet. The lecture concludes with a reflection on the duality of human nature, using the events of September 11 as a symbol of the tension between destructive impulses and collective solidarity. Pesce urges the students, as future leaders, to recognize the ethical responsibilities that accompany their privileged positions and the immense power they will hold in shaping this technological future.
