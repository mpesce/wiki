# piracyisgood.mp3

## Summary

This recording is a lecture delivered by Mark Pesce, a media and technology commentator, at an event in Australia, likely the Australian Film, Television and Radio School (AFTRS), given his affiliation and the specific focus on the Australian broadcast market. The talk, titled "Piracy is Good," addresses the disruption of traditional television economics by file-sharing technologies, specifically BitTorrent. Pesce argues that piracy is not an evil to be fought but a natural audience behavior that can be harnessed for economic gain. He illustrates this with case studies of Battlestar Galactica and Doctor Who, noting that unauthorized distribution in the UK and Canada actually boosted ratings in the US and UK, respectively, by generating word-of-mouth buzz among core fans. Pesce explains that BitTorrent creates a "swarm" where popularity increases distribution efficiency, reversing the traditional server-load problem. He critiques the Australian television oligopoly, where three commercial networks maintain high profits by buying foreign content cheaply and selling advertising slots at a premium due to limited competition. He proposes a new "hyperdistribution" model where producers partner directly with advertisers, bypassing broadcasters. In this model, advertising is embedded subtly within the program stream, such as in the on-screen bug, rather than in traditional breaks. Pesce demonstrates this concept by showing a modified Battlestar Galactica clip with a retailer’s logo. He argues that this approach is more cost-effective for producers and advertisers, allowing for global distribution and higher returns per viewer. The lecture concludes with practical advice for producers, emphasizing that shorter, funnier content is better suited for fragmented viewing habits across various devices. Pesce predicts that while broadcast networks will retain control over live events like sports and news, pre-produced drama will shift to a fragmented, advertiser-funded model. He addresses objections regarding copyright and technical difficulty, asserting that the technology is already evolving to support non-infringing uses and that the interface will improve. He predicts that a successful hyper-distributed television program will emerge before the end of the year, marking a significant shift in the industry.
