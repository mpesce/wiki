# ALA.wav

## Summary

This recording is a technology talk delivered by Mark Pesce, who introduces himself as a former Apple and Mozilla engineer and media commentator. The presentation traces the history of virtual reality and augmented reality, beginning with the 1950s jet fighter cockpit, where instrumentation complexity exceeded pilot capacity, leading to the need for synthesized information. Pesce highlights the role of DARPA in funding early research, specifically noting Ivan Sutherland’s 1963 creation of Sketchpad, the first interactive computer program, and his subsequent development of the first head-mounted display with positional tracking. The talk covers the evolution of VR hardware, including the NASA Ames Research Center’s virtual environment workstation from the late 1980s, which used data gloves and a million-dollar supercomputer, and the commercial failure of Nintendo’s Power Glove in 1989 due to poor user testing. Pesce also discusses his own startup’s work with Sega on the Virtua VR, which was cancelled due to motion sickness issues, and his later development of VRML, a 3D interface for the World Wide Web that laid the groundwork for modern web graphics. The second half of the talk focuses on the resurgence of VR between 2014 and 2016, driven by Moore’s Law and improved hardware. Pesce introduces the concept of videogrammetry, defined as photogrammetry plus a timeline, which allows for the capture of dynamic 3D models from video. He demonstrates this technology using examples from Eight Eye, a company that uses a dome of 40 high-definition cameras to capture human performance, and a PS VR app featuring violinist Joshua Bell, which uses positional tracking to place the viewer within the performance space. The talk concludes with a demonstration of Microsoft Research’s polyportation technology, which enables real-time 3D telepresence by compositing live video feeds into a shared virtual space. Pesce argues that the cost of this technology is dropping rapidly, citing consumer-grade depth cameras like the Kinect and Intel RealSense, and predicts that videogrammetry will become a standard tool for media production and broadcasting within the next five years.
