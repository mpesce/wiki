# VR Creative Cluster Mark Pesce 9 Aug video1_er.MP4

## Summary

This recording is a talk delivered by Mark Pesce, a pioneer in consumer virtual reality and web technologies, to an audience in Melbourne. Pesce recounts the history of VR from the early 1990s to the present day, beginning with his founding of Ono Sendai, the first consumer VR company in the UK. He details how his team reduced the cost of orientation tracking sensors from $50 to $1 by combining automotive components, a technology they licensed to Sega. Although the Sega VR headset was a hit at the 1993 Consumer Electronics Show, it was cancelled after user testing revealed that users experienced motion sickness, marking the end of the first VR era. Following the cancellation, Pesce shifted his focus to the emerging World Wide Web. In 1993, he and Tony Parisi developed the first VRML browser, demonstrating a 3D model known as the cyber banana. Pesce presented this work at the first international conference on the World Wide Web, where he met Tim Berners-Lee and other key figures. This collaboration led to VRML becoming a standard, supported by SGI, and Pesce established a consultancy called Vorkom to explore streaming 3D media. However, VRML eventually stagnated due to high hardware costs, the lack of native browser support, and the public’s unfamiliarity with real-time 3D graphics. Pesce argues that the current resurgence of VR is driven by several converging factors. He notes that computer graphics are now 1,000 times faster than in 1996, and the proliferation of smartphones has made high-quality displays and sensors inexpensive. Additionally, two decades of exposure to 3D gaming have normalized the technology for the general public. Pesce highlights the rapid drop in hardware prices, noting that a high-end VR setup has decreased from approximately $5,000 to $2,000 in just six months. He concludes with a warning regarding the upcoming PlayStation VR launch, cautioning that if early experiences are poor or cause motion sickness, the industry risks alienating a new generation of users.
