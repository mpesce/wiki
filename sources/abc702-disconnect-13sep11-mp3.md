# abc702-disconnect-13Sep11.mp3

## Summary

This recording is a radio interview featuring Mark Pesce, a media and technology commentator, discussing his work on a book about digital disconnection. The host, identified as James, opens the segment with a lighthearted anecdote about Pesce’s habit of repeatedly pressing pedestrian crossing buttons in Sydney, which Pesce admits to doing out of a need for control. The conversation then shifts to the main topic: the rapid adoption of smartphones in Australia, which Pesce notes reached 50 percent penetration in 2011, making it the first major country to achieve such widespread connectivity. He argues that this constant connection has eroded the sense of solitude and internal reflection, particularly for younger generations who have never known a disconnected life. Pesce introduces the concept of "connection hygiene," comparing the need for digital boundaries to nutritional guidelines. He cites the example of a couple in California who practice a "technology Sabbath," turning off all devices from Friday sundown to Saturday sundown to reconnect with their immediate surroundings. The discussion highlights that while connectivity is comforting, it often replaces meaningful interaction with the physical world, a phenomenon listeners compare to the social isolation caused by Walkman headphones in the 1980s. Callers share personal experiences, including a parent struggling to set limits for teenage children who are heavily engaged in programming and social media, and another noting the difficulty of engaging teenagers in household conversations when they are absorbed in their devices. The interview concludes with advice on managing these dynamics. Pesce suggests that families should approach disconnection collaboratively, viewing it as a positive experience rather than a punishment. He emphasizes that the goal is not to ban technology but to create a balanced continuum where individuals can choose between being connected to others, connected to themselves, or fully present in the room. He notes that while academic guidelines are still emerging, the crucial first step is for individuals and families to start the conversation about how to reclaim private, disconnected time.
