# artbell2.mp3

## Summary

This recording is a segment from the Art Bell radio program, featuring an interview with Mark Pesce, a media and technology commentator. The broadcast begins with a news bulletin from Richard C. Hoagland regarding alleged military activity in Antarctica. Hoagland reports that a source, an independent truck driver hired by the military, stated he was tasked with moving 20-foot boxes from Miramar to Antarctica in a hurry. The boxes reportedly contain GPS transmitters for tracking. This report is paralleled by claims from a Norwegian source that significant equipment has been flown into the Russian Bostock base for six months, suggesting a major, undisclosed event is occurring in the region. Following a commercial break, the focus shifts to an interview with Mark Pesce about artificial intelligence and robotics. Pesce discusses the MIT projects Kismet and COG, explaining that they are designed to learn through interaction with their environment, a concept based on the theories of psychologist Jean Piaget. He argues that embodiment is crucial for developing intelligence, as physical dexterity and interaction allow machines to understand the world. Pesce uses the commercial toy Furby as an example of how interactive machines are already influencing human behavior and perception, noting that children view such devices as neither inanimate objects nor living animals, but something in between. The conversation then explores the ethical implications of creating superhuman intelligence. Pesce suggests that while computers may eventually surpass human intelligence, the primary danger may not be a hostile takeover, but rather the inability of humans to communicate with or control such an entity. He proposes that research into advanced intelligence might eventually require containment similar to biological clean rooms to prevent unintended consequences. The segment concludes with a discussion of nanotechnology and the "gray goo" scenario, a theoretical catastrophe where self-replicating nanobots consume all organic and inorganic matter on Earth within 72 hours. Pesce notes that this technology is potentially 10 to 15 years away and highlights the work of the Foresight Organization, which addresses the ethical dimensions of such emerging technologies.
