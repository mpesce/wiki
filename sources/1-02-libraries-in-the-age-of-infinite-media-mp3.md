# 1-02 Libraries in the Age of Infinite Media.mp3

## Summary

This recording is a lecture or talk delivered by Mark Pesce, a media and technology commentator, addressing an audience that appears to include librarians and media professionals. The talk focuses on the meta-trends of media digitization, tracing the evolution from physical formats to digital distribution. Pesce begins by discussing the separation of media from its physical form starting in 1982 with the compact disc, noting that the consequences of digitization, such as the iPod, were not predictable in advance. He breaks down the last decade of media distribution into three periods, starting with the early 1990s era of "real media," where streaming audio and video were low-quality but represented a significant improvement over nothing. He highlights the infrastructure costs associated with bandwidth, using the ABC and SBS in Australia as contrasting examples of how broadcasters handle traffic. The second period discussed is the "era of super distribution," initiated in 1999 by Sean Fanning’s Napster. Pesce describes Napster as a tool that allowed users to share music files, creating a massive, albeit temporary, centralized database of personal hard drives. While it facilitated access to obscure recordings for researchers, it was ultimately shut down by the recording industry for copyright violations. This led to the rise of more decentralized, harder-to-trace file-sharing networks. The third period is defined by the emergence of BitTorrent in 2002, developed by programmer Bram Cohen. Pesce explains BitTorrent’s efficiency in distributing large files by breaking them into pieces shared among a swarm of users, which has profoundly impacted the film and television industries. He notes that broadcasters are adapting by offering downloadable content to compete with piracy, effectively turning the internet into a global video on demand service. Pesce then shifts focus to the "amateur revolution," citing YouTube as a prime example of user-generated content that has surpassed traditional media sites in popularity. He argues that the true battle is not between professionals and pirates, but between professional and amateur producers, as distribution costs have evaporated. This has led to the formation of micro-audiences rather than mass audiences. The talk concludes with a discussion on the role of librarians in this new landscape, suggesting they must become "guardians of chaos" to help users navigate infinite content. Pesce emphasizes the importance of Wikipedia as a de facto public reference work, urging institutions to maintain and contribute to it. He also touches on Web 2.0 concepts like social bookmarking (Delicious), social news (Digg), and social networking (MySpace), arguing that the future belongs to those who are agile and willing to share.
