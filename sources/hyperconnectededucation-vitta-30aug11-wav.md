# HyperconnectedEducation-VITTA-30Aug11.wav

## Summary

This recording is a lecture delivered by Mark Pesce, a media and technology commentator, at the University of Sydney, where he holds an honorary position. The talk, which appears to have taken place in 2011 based on references to recent events and surveys, focuses on the rapid integration of mobile technology into the lives of children and the subsequent impact on education and parenting. Pesce begins by presenting data from an ethnographic survey of 80 university students, noting that the average age of first mobile phone ownership has dropped from 11 in 2003 to between eight and nine years old today. He argues that as device costs decrease, mobile ownership is becoming inevitable for all children, transforming the phone from a communication tool into a tether for parental surveillance. Pesce discusses the rise of "helicopter parenting," where technology allows parents to maintain constant vigilance over their children’s activities and safety. He cites an anecdote from a South Korean classroom where children viewed GPS tracking as a sign of parental care rather than intrusion. He also describes the emerging market for child-friendly devices, such as spy watches and potential future embedded tags, which will allow parents to monitor their children’s physical condition and environment in real-time. While acknowledging the benefits of safety, he raises concerns about the psychological impact of such constant connectivity, referencing psychologist Sherry Turkle’s research on how electronic tethers may inhibit a child’s development of independence and boundary exploration. The lecture concludes with an examination of the changing role of educators in a pervasively connected culture. Pesce notes that while knowledge is freely available through platforms like Khan Academy and YouTube, the human element of mentorship remains essential. He highlights the challenge for teachers, who now face students who are directly connected to hyper-vigilant parents, complicating classroom discipline. To address the need for professional development, he introduces a winning project from a recent Sydney Startup Weekend called Class Meet, a platform designed to facilitate peer-to-peer connection and collaboration among educators. Pesce asserts that the current decade is a critical period for making institutional and pedagogical adjustments to accommodate a society where being human and being connected are becoming synonymous.
