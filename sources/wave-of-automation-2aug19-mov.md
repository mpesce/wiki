# Wave of Automation 2Aug19.mov

## Summary

This recording is a lecture delivered by Mark Pesce, a media and technology commentator, to an audience at Radford College. The talk, which references a 2019 context due to the mention of the Fortnite World Cup winner Kyle Giersdorf and current events like the New York Times undercover food delivery report, explores the intersection of automation, education, and future employment. Pesce begins by contrasting historical business failures, such as Blockbuster and Tower Records, with the rise of digital platforms, noting that while material wealth has peaked in Australia, the definition of success is shifting. He argues that the common narrative that robots will replace all human labor is flawed; instead, automation is currently targeting high-value professions like law, finance, and medicine, while physically demanding and unpredictable roles, such as food delivery, remain difficult to automate. Pesce uses the example of Tesla’s Model 3 production line to illustrate that human "soft skills" and dexterity are still superior to robotics in many contexts. He posits that the value of human labor lies in its resistance to automation, not in the cost of the education required to acquire it. Citing AT&T’s strategy of continuous staff upskilling, he suggests that the traditional model of education as a one-time event is obsolete. The future requires a seamless transition between school and work, where learning is a continuous, lifelong process. He criticizes the current disconnect between formal education and workplace needs, using Telstra’s simultaneous job cuts and hiring as an example of inefficient organizational planning. The central thesis of the talk is that mentoring is the core skill for the 21st century. Pesce argues that the ability to negotiate learning environments, mentor others, and be mentored is the foundational human capacity that allows individuals and organizations to thrive amidst technological change. He urges educators to focus on relational skills and peer mentoring from an early age, framing education not as a culmination but as a continuation of a lifelong journey. The lecture concludes with the assertion that by leveraging this ancient human capacity for mutual learning, society can build a culture that thrives in an age of automation.
