## Summary

This recording is a lecture delivered by a researcher at the University of Sydney, likely around 2009, addressing educators in Australia. The speaker argues that the current era is defined by a "gigantic orgy of sharing," a cultural shift driven by hyperconnectivity that is fundamentally altering education. He posits that while sharing physical objects is a learned behavior, sharing digital bits is innate, a distinction previously noted by Nicholas Negroponte. The talk examines how this innate desire to share has disrupted traditional industries, citing Napster, Wikipedia, and RateMyProfessors as examples of collective intelligence that have shifted power balances and destabilized established institutions. The speaker identifies three converging megatrends that will transform Australian education over the next decade. First, the National Broadband Network (NBN) will provide unmetered, high-speed internet, ending the era of rationed connectivity and enabling new innovations. Second, the government’s initiative to provide computers to students in years 9 through 12 will alter classroom dynamics, though the speaker warns that without proper digital citizenship education, these devices may simply become distractions or tools for misuse. Third, the upcoming national curriculum, expected to be implemented in 2011, offers a unique opportunity. Rather than viewing the curriculum as a rigid mandate, the speaker urges educators to treat it as an organizing principle for sharing. He envisions a future where teachers and students use the curriculum as a "string" to connect with shared resources, lesson plans, and peer communities across the country, turning the classroom from a solitary island into a connected node within a larger network. The lecture concludes with a warning against defaulting to industrial-era educational models where computers serve merely as electronic textbooks. The speaker highlights emerging models like StraighterLine, which offers low-cost, online tertiary education, as a preview of the competition facing traditional schools. He calls for a change in intent, urging the educational community to formalize the sharing of knowledge and experience.
