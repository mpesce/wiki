## Summary

This recording is a presentation by Mark Pesce, facilitated by Gary, at an event likely focused on education and media technology. Pesce, identified as the inventor of the Virtual Reality Modeling Language and author of five books, discusses the impact of peer-produced knowledge sharing on society and education. He frames the discussion around the constructivist view that changing how people acquire knowledge fundamentally alters how they act and understand the world. Pesce compares four different knowledge repositories using the entry for "kangaroo" as a case study to illustrate the spectrum of authority and trust. He contrasts Wikipedia, which relies on distributed authority and community interaction, with Britannica, which offers a centralized, paid model of expert authority. He also examines Citizenium, a project by a Wikipedia co-founder that attempts to introduce stricter editorial review, noting its slower growth compared to Wikipedia. Finally, he highlights Conservapedia and Uncyclopedia, which represent special interests and intentional satire, respectively, demonstrating how different groups shape knowledge to fit specific narratives or agendas. The speaker argues that the rise of pervasive peer production creates three conflicting forces: elites who resist the distribution of knowledge to maintain power, special interests that manipulate content to serve their aims, and communities that are beginning to harness their latent knowledge. Pesce predicts that as these technologies enter schools and organizations, debates over truth, trust, and authority will become increasingly heated. He advises attendees to be aware of these natural barriers and to proactively engage with stakeholders who may feel disempowered by the shift toward open knowledge sharing. The talk concludes with a call to navigate these tensions as peer-produced knowledge becomes a central part of the cultural and educational landscape.
