# ARIN 2610 Internet Transformations The AR Dilemmas 5Oct21 720p.mp4

## Summary

This recording is a lecture delivered by Mark Pesce, a media and technology commentator and former Apple and Mozilla engineer. The talk focuses on the definition, history, and societal implications of augmented reality (AR). Pesce begins by recounting the 2016 "riot" in Rhodes, Australia, where thousands of people gathered in Peg Patterson Park to play Pokemon Go, illustrating how inscribing data into physical space alters human behavior. He argues that AR is not merely a technological apparatus like head-mounted displays, but a fundamental human activity of binding data to specific locations, a practice dating back to ancient cave paintings. The speaker highlights the ethical and legal challenges of this technology, specifically the "right to write" in shared spaces. He notes that companies like Facebook, Google, and Apple are asserting the ability to place digital content in the physical world without consent, creating a contested reality. Pesce also addresses privacy concerns, describing AR devices as tools of networked surveillance. He cites Facebook’s Project Aria and the use of gaze detection to track user attention, arguing that these systems will create a panopticon where personal data is continuously harvested and used to manipulate engagement, similar to the negative engagement algorithms revealed in recent whistleblower reports. In the final section, Pesce outlines the potential benefits of AR, particularly regarding transparency and sustainability. He uses the example of modern automobiles, where manufacturers obscure engine data to maintain control over maintenance, to argue that AR could empower users by revealing hidden metadata about their devices and energy consumption. This transparency is essential for managing carbon emissions and achieving a sustainable economy. The lecture concludes with a reference to the 1995 TVision project, a virtual globe system that offered a more ambitious vision of data-rich interaction than current tools like Google Earth, urging designers to consider the full depth of data representation in future AR systems.
