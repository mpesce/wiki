# The Human Use of Human Networks.mp3

## Summary

This recording is a presentation by Mark Pesce, a media and technology commentator, discussing the convergence of entertainment creation and technology utilization. Speaking from his role at AFTRS, Pesce outlines the themes of his upcoming book, Hyper People, which explores the intersection of peer-to-peer file sharing, social networks, and personal media culture. He argues that these technologies are revolutionizing consumer bandwidth adoption and shifting control of content distribution from broadcasters to individual users. Pesce illustrates this shift with the "Doctor Who Effect," where a leaked episode was widely distributed via BitTorrent, demonstrating the loss of broadcaster control and the rise of hyper-distribution. He notes that this disintermediation removes traditional distribution channels, creating both economic challenges for producers and opportunities for global reach. Pesce addresses the resulting "decision paralysis" caused by unlimited content choices, proposing active digital social networks as a solution. He critiques current social platforms as static and needy of user attention, arguing instead for dynamic networks that function as knowledge amplifiers. He suggests integrating social networks with media consumption data from devices like iTunes and TiVo to provide personalized recommendations based on friends' viewing habits. Furthermore, he envisions using social networks as spam filters and tools for "knowledge amplification," allowing users to query their social circles for understanding rather than just facts. Pesce references the Dunbar number, suggesting that digital prostheses could help humans manage social networks larger than the biological limit of 150 relationships. The talk concludes with a Q&A session where Pesce discusses the differences between social credit and algorithmic importance, the need for distributed social network architectures to protect privacy, and the adoption of social tools by older generations and corporations. He also touches on parental monitoring of children's online activities and the economic models needed to sustain professional content creation in an era of free distribution, inviting the audience to an AFTRS event on May 6th to discuss these economic proposals.
