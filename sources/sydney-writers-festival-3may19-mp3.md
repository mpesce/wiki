# Sydney Writers Festival 3May19.mp3

## Summary

This recording is a panel discussion titled Digital Disruption, hosted by the Griffith Review at the Sydney Writers Festival. The speakers include futurist Mark Pesce, Indigenous academic Bronwyn Carlson, and former Green Senator Scott Ludlam. The conversation explores the data economy, digital rights, and the societal impacts of social media and artificial intelligence. Mark Pesce opens by describing his personal decision to take a break from Twitter after years of heavy use, citing the platform’s toxicity and his need for emotional relief. He discusses the psychological strain of being connected to a massive, unfiltered stream of global events and anger. Bronwyn Carlson then shifts the focus to the positive uses of social media by Indigenous Australian communities. She highlights the IndigenousX Twitter handle as a tool for amplifying Indigenous voices and countering mainstream media narratives. Carlson also describes using social media feeds as teaching tools for students to confront historical and contemporary injustices, framing these digital interactions as a modern "midden" that records cultural connections and conflicts. Scott Ludlam addresses the history of the internet, contrasting its early open-source, collaborative ethos with its current state of privatization and surveillance capitalism. He advocates for tools that protect privacy and rebuild the digital commons, such as DuckDuckGo, and criticizes the extractive nature of data collection. The panel then discusses artificial intelligence, with Carlson noting that fear of robots is culturally specific and that the real concern lies in the moral codes embedded by designers, particularly regarding facial recognition bias against Indigenous peoples. Pesce and Ludlam examine the European General Data Protection Regulation and Australia’s Access and Assistance Act, analyzing how legislation attempts to balance government access with user privacy. The discussion concludes with Pesce advocating for digital sabbaths to reclaim attention, while Carlson notes that for many Indigenous communities, the internet is a new and ongoing transition rather than a ubiquitous constant.
