# Loreto.mp3

## Summary

This recording is a talk by futurist and technology commentator Mark Pesce, delivered to an audience of educators. The speaker notes that he entered first grade fifty years prior to the talk, which he uses as a framing device to discuss the shift from linear life paths to flexible, lifelong learning models. Pesce argues that the current cultural fear of artificial intelligence replacing human jobs is exaggerated, comparing the hype around AI demos to the "Wizard of Oz." He uses examples such as self-driving cars struggling with complex urban environments like Sydney’s George Street, and the game of Go, to illustrate that while machines excel at regular tasks, humans remain essential for handling exceptions. He predicts that by 2033, the average worker will spend half their time learning new skills and half practicing existing ones, a trend already visible in corporate retraining programs at companies like AT&T. The core of the presentation focuses on mentoring as the cornerstone of future education and work. Pesce advocates for a transition from traditional, industrial-style didactic teaching to a peer-oriented culture where knowledge sharing is continuous. He suggests that peer mentoring and peer assessment should be introduced in early childhood to help students navigate a world where the "goalposts are moving." He emphasizes that this approach is not new, citing historical examples of knowledge sharing, but argues it must be formalized to prepare students for a post-industrial economy. The talk includes interactive segments where Pesce engages with the audience on practical classroom challenges, such as the tension between rigid timetabling and flexible learning, and the resistance parents may feel toward decentralized educational models. Pesce concludes by revisiting the story of Lee Sedol, the Go grandmaster who lost to AlphaGo but subsequently used the AI to improve his own skills, reaching new personal highs. He introduces the concept of "pair play," where a human and a computer team outperform a computer alone, demonstrating that technology amplifies human exceptionalism rather than replacing it. The speaker frames the future not as a race between humans and machines, but as a partnership where humans leverage AI tools to achieve unprecedented levels of capability. He urges educators to view their role as guides who facilitate these peer networks, ensuring that students develop the relational and expressive skills necessary to thrive in a rapidly changing world.
