# mindstates-naturalselection.mp4

## Summary

This recording is a lecture or talk delivered by Mark Pesce, a media and technology commentator, to an audience at a conference. The speaker opens with a humorous remark about the audience potentially being under the influence of acid before launching into a discussion on the biological and social implications of natural selection. He begins by explaining the mechanism of antibiotic resistance in bacteria, specifically detailing how the misuse of antibiotics for tuberculosis has led to the emergence of extremely drug-resistant TB, or XDR TB. He uses this as a case study to illustrate how selection pressure forces organisms to adapt, noting that resistant strains are often stronger rather than weaker. The narrative then shifts to the history of evolutionary theory, focusing on Alfred Russel Wallace. Pesce recounts how Wallace, while suffering from malaria in the Malay Archipelago in 1854, conceived of the theory of natural selection. He highlights the discovery of the Wallace Line, which separates distinct biogeographic regions, and explains how Wallace’s feverish delirium helped him understand that small variations could lead to the replacement of species over time. The speaker contrasts Wallace’s lack of social prestige with that of Charles Darwin, arguing that Darwin’s family wealth and social standing were necessary to get the theory accepted by the scientific establishment and the Church of England. Pesce posits that the idea of natural selection survived because it was well-adapted to the scientific environment, much like biological organisms. In the final section, Pesce applies the concept of natural selection to military history and modern conflict. He discusses the First World War, describing it as a period of intense selection pressure where outdated tactics like cavalry charges were eliminated by machine guns and trench warfare. He extends this analysis to the contemporary wars in Iraq and Afghanistan, arguing that the use of overwhelming force by the United States acts as a selection pressure that accelerates the adaptation of insurgent groups. He notes that modern insurgents use the internet to rapidly share successful tactics, making them more resilient. The talk concludes with a quote from Lao Tzu’s Tao Te Ching, suggesting that violence rebounds upon itself and that the 21st century is entering a "Taoist" era where these ancient philosophical principles are being recognized as physical laws.
