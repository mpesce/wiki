# 3-03 Empire.mp3

## Summary

This recording is a talk by Mark Pesce, a media and technology commentator, delivered to a San Francisco audience. The presentation, titled "Empire part 1: Houses of stone," reflects on a recent trip around the world where Pesce followed the sun from Sydney to Pisa, Rome, and across the western United States. He begins by recounting an epiphany in the Pisa Cathedral, where he felt a sudden, holistic understanding of the architecture and history, contrasting this hushed experience with the overwhelming grandeur of St. Peter’s Basilica in Rome. In Rome, he discusses the continuity of the Roman Empire, arguing that power evolved from physical force to spiritual authority, symbolized by Michelangelo’s Pieta and the bones of St. Peter. Pesce then describes driving the US West Coast from San Diego to Seattle, highlighting the sensory richness of the Oregon coast and the temperate rainforest of the Olympic Peninsula. He critiques the environmental impact of clear-cut logging in national forests, using the metaphor of "beautiful motherfucking clear cuts" to discuss media saturation. He suggests that just as the wind of the sea can drown out the motion of a boat, the constant noise of mass media can obscure deeper cultural realities. He references a friend who avoids news consumption to maintain clarity, linking this to the need for an "ecology of mass media." The talk concludes with anecdotes from Seattle, including a drunken discussion with friend James Kent about a new theory on how psychedelics work, which involves a feedback loop between perception and cognition. Pesce also shares a conversation with a young woman who identifies as a humanist rather than a transhumanist, using this to explore the tension between mind and body. He ends by describing a party in Seattle, celebrating the community of "freaks" who thrive in the liminal spaces between established structures, asserting that while all "castles in the air" eventually fall, the human experience remains valid in the present moment.
