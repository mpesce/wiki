# TheNextBillionSecondsInEducation-1Sep11.wav

## Summary

This recording captures a keynote address by Mark Pesce, a media commentator, writer, and former Apple and Mozilla engineer, at an Australian education conference. Pesce, who co-invented VRML in 1994 and has written five books including The Playful World, discusses the transformative impact of hyper-connectivity on society and education. He notes that he is currently writing his sixth book, The Next Billion Seconds, which argues that the period between 1995 and 2026 will be as significant to the human species as the birth of language. The talk appears to have taken place around 2010 or 2011, as Pesce references a 2010 survey regarding school administrators and mentions that the mobile phone was introduced 30 years prior in 1981. Pesce begins by analyzing the declining age of first mobile phone ownership, noting that while his 2003 survey of university students showed an average age of 11.5, current data suggests children are obtaining devices by age eight. He argues that as smartphone prices drop, the device is becoming a ubiquitous sensor for parents, enabling constant monitoring of children’s location and well-being. This trend leads to "helicopter parenting" via technology, which psychologist Sherry Turkle warns may hinder a child’s development of independence. Pesce illustrates this with anecdotes about GPS-tracked students in South Korea and a hypothetical scenario where a parent monitors a bullied child via a live video feed from a collar-mounted camera. He asserts that schools can no longer ban these devices and must instead adapt their pedagogy to this connected reality. The latter portion of the talk focuses on the implications for educators. Pesce emphasizes that while content is freely available through resources like Wikipedia and Khan Academy, the human element of mentoring remains crucial. He advocates for teacher-to-teacher sharing of lesson plans, referencing the Australian national curriculum as a potential organizing principle for this exchange. He critiques existing platforms like the Victorian Department’s "Alternet" for being unfriendly to users and highlights the need for better tools that allow educators to share resources and receive professional development. Pesce concludes by urging educators to teach "hygiene of connectivity," helping students manage their connections and value disconnection, and arguing that assessment must shift from testing retention to evaluating a student’s ability to research and master new topics using connected devices. The recording ends with a Q&A session where Pesce discusses intellectual property, the quality of shared resources, and the future of testing.
