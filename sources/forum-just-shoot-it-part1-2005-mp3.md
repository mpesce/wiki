# forum-just-shoot-it-part1-2005.mp3

## Summary

This recording captures a panel discussion from the "Just Shoot It" forum, held on March 17, 2005, at the Australian Centre for the Moving Image in Melbourne. The event, a joint project between Spinach 7 Digital and Busa Art, featured three speakers: Mark Pesci, Shannon Owen, and David Tylee. The central theme was the challenge of funding and distributing independent video production in an era of shifting media landscapes. Mark Pesci opened by arguing that Australian commercial television networks are unviable partners for independent creators because they purchase American programming at a fraction of its production cost, generating massive advertising revenue without supporting local content. He noted that Australians download more television over the internet than any other nation, indicating a readiness for alternative distribution. Pesci highlighted the upcoming release of the PlayStation Portable as a device that would revolutionize video consumption, similar to how the iPod changed audio, by freeing content from fixed locations and times. He advocated for creators to bypass traditional broadcasters and distributors by forging direct relationships with communities and advertisers. He cited the film Outfoxed as a successful model where production was funded by political organizations and distributed through social networks, allowing for direct audience engagement and revenue generation without intermediary gatekeepers. Shannon Owen countered Pesci’s optimism by raising concerns about the digital divide. She argued that while emerging media technologies are more accessible, they remain gatekept by money and education. Her work often involves marginalized communities, such as Aboriginal elders in remote areas or refugees, who lack the infrastructure or skills to access digital platforms. She emphasized the continued necessity of traditional television for reaching these audiences and achieving broad cultural impact. David Tylee added that while he supports the potential of new media, the Australian documentary community has historically struggled with a cultural aversion to commercial sponsorship, unlike the United States. He noted that while DVD and internet distribution offer stability and authorial control, they often result in one-off successes rather than sustainable institutional support. The discussion concluded with a consensus that while traditional broadcasting is failing to serve independent creators, the future lies in embracing fragmented audiences and direct distribution methods, though the challenge of sustaining a viable economic model remains significant.
