# ConnectedCity-2Aug11.AVI

## Summary

This recording features a talk by Mark Pesce, a media and technology commentator, addressing the implications of hyper-connectivity for urban governance and resident life in Sydney. Speaking from his perspective as a long-time Sydney resident who moved from Los Angeles in 1997, Pesce contrasts the low mobile phone ownership rates of that era with the current 120 percent subscription rate in Australia. He argues that the mobile phone has transformed the city from a vast, unknowable entity into a portable, personal resource, allowing residents to bypass traditional organizational hierarchies and access information or help directly. This shift has created a dense network of individual connections that now serves as the primary channel for news and situational awareness, often superseding official city sources. Pesce contends that because residents now share information rapidly, the city can no longer rely on being the sole authority on urban matters. Instead, it must adapt by making its data easily shareable and by actively engaging with the community networks where residents are already exchanging expertise. He highlights that every resident possesses hidden knowledge and skills that become visible through sharing, creating a vast, organic knowledge network. To manage this, the city must also enhance its internal capabilities, ensuring that every employee has access to the collective experience of the entire workforce. This internal empowerment is necessary to match the growing power of residents, who will increasingly demand their needs be met and may conflict with city authorities if their expectations are not managed. The talk concludes with a warning that while this connectedness offers radical empowerment, it also risks social fragmentation if the city fails to keep pace. Pesce suggests that the city must step in to shape and facilitate this sharing for the public good. Following the main presentation, a brief Q&A session occurs where a moderator discusses the political implications of viral networks and the importance of transparency. Another speaker, identified as Terry, describes a crowdsourcing project involving people with disabilities reporting infrastructure issues, noting that city departments are currently ill-equipped to handle direct, transparent feedback from empowered citizens.
