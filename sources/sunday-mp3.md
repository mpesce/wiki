## Summary

This recording captures a group discussion session from a seminar, likely held at the Esalen Institute, facilitated by Mark Pesce. The session begins with participants sharing reflections from the previous night’s events, which included a yoga session involving chakra tones and a ritualistic gathering. Pesce opens by discussing his personal history with spiritual frauds, explaining how his skepticism toward "new age" energy practices stems from a past experience where a supposed master was revealed to be a con artist. He argues that this background trained him to avoid becoming a guru himself and to prioritize media literacy and transparency. Despite his skepticism, Pesce describes a profound moment during the previous night’s ritual where he felt a collective resonance, leading him to conclude that God exists but is only conscious through group interaction. He draws parallels between this experience and Jewish traditions, emphasizing the importance of community, text-based inquiry, and deconstruction over individual faith. The conversation shifts to the tension between spiritual experience and social action. Participants debate whether spiritual practices should lead to political engagement or remain focused on immediate, personal transformation. Pesce and others argue that while meditation and ecstatic experiences are valuable, they must generate substantive change in the real world to avoid becoming mere entertainment or escapism. They discuss the limitations of individual action, such as reducing SUV usage, versus the power of institutional change and collective activism. The group analyzes the anti-globalization movement as a form of "spectacular refusal," noting its heterogeneous nature and its use of independent media networks to bypass traditional corporate control. They explore how technology facilitates the formation of smaller, fluid affinity groups or "tribes" that can sustain community in an increasingly individualistic and virtual world. The final segment addresses the relationship between technology, science, and spirituality. A participant asks if future computers can measure the soul or prove the existence of God. Pesce responds that technology tends to absorb the mysterious, turning spiritual concepts into measurable objects, which only pushes the frontier of mystery further out. He suggests that religion is more akin to technology than science, serving as a practical tool for organizing experience rather than a set of verifiable facts. The discussion concludes with the idea that while science may never fully capture the spiritual, the integration of technological metaphors with spiritual practices creates a dynamic space for ongoing exploration and social engagement.
