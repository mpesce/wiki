# 1_DIA_MarkPesce.mp3

## Summary

This recording is a talk by Mark Pesce, a media and technology commentator, likely delivered at a conference in New Zealand given references to local institutions like Te Papa and Radio NZ. The presentation focuses on the evolution of social media and its profound impact on organizational structures and individual behavior. Pesce begins by discussing the limitations of human social capacity, referencing the concept of Dunbar's number, and explains how platforms like Facebook and Twitter serve as mechanisms to manage acquaintances and share media. He contrasts generic social networks with specialized tools like LinkedIn and Yammer, noting that highly tuned networks for specific communities are more effective. He also highlights the rise of social messaging and knowledge sharing, citing Wikipedia and WikiLeaks as examples of agreements to share information, and introduces Aardvark as a social search engine acquired by Google. A significant portion of the talk addresses the destabilizing effects of social media on organizations. Pesce argues that information flows no longer respect organizational hierarchies or rules, creating pressure from both inside and outside the organization to connect and engage. He suggests that traditional message discipline is insufficient and instead advocates for guidelines, such as those developed by the Australian Broadcasting Corporation, which emphasize not mixing professional and personal lives in ways that bring disrepute, not undermining work effectiveness, not implying organizational endorsement of personal views, and not disclosing confidential information. He stresses the importance of authenticity and forgiveness in this new era of hyperconnectivity. In the latter half, Pesce shifts to the future of mobile technology and augmented reality. He describes how smartphones with cameras, GPS, and compasses can act as magnifying glasses to reveal hidden data about physical objects. He provides a practical example of scanning a packet of meat in a supermarket to access traceability data regarding animal health, nutritional content, and carbon footprint. He argues that this capability requires government agencies to release and connect various datasets, such as health and safety records, to enable individuals to make informed decisions. The talk concludes with a Q&A session where Pesce discusses the timeline for smartphone adoption, the necessity of explicit data privacy arrangements, and the commercial implications of traceability for businesses.
