## Summary

This recording is a conference talk by Mark Pesce titled "Using the Network for Business Success: Part One, My How Things Have Changed." Pesce, a media and technology commentator, outlines the evolution of business communication in Australia from the telephone and fax to the internet and social media. He notes that while email became the dominant business tool in the late 1990s, the rise of Web 2.0 transformed the web into a two-way medium for interaction. Pesce argues that social networks like Facebook and Twitter act as "supercharged Rolodexes," allowing individuals to maintain connections beyond the natural cognitive limit of 150 relationships. He emphasizes that sharing content strengthens bonds but also creates a burden of monitoring multiple channels. Pesce illustrates how hyper-connectivity has rewritten the relationship between businesses and customers. He cites a positive example where a user’s favorable tweet about Virgin Australia’s premium economy service reached thousands of potential customers, yet the airline failed to engage because it was distracted by a poorly received marketing campaign. Conversely, he describes a negative scenario where a customer named Kate Carruthers publicly vented on Twitter after American Express declined her card without notice. Because Amex did not monitor social media, the complaint spread to her thousands of followers, damaging the brand’s reputation. Pesce argues that traditional mass marketing is ineffective in this new landscape, which relies on authenticity and peer-to-peer conversation rather than paid advertising. In his advice to businesses, Pesce urges organizations to stop blocking social media on corporate networks and instead trust employees to use these platforms responsibly. He suggests that small and medium-sized businesses should empower their entire workforce to monitor online conversations, acting as the company’s "eyes and ears" to identify both praise and potential crises. He references the Australian Broadcasting Corporation’s simple social media guidelines as a model for establishing mutual respect between employers and employees. Pesce concludes that social media is not a fad but the new standard for business, requiring a shift from broadcast-style advertising to genuine, ongoing dialogue with customers. The session ends with a brief Q&A regarding monitoring tools and the use of social media in educational training contexts.
