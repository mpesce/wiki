# whereintheworld.mp3

## Summary

This recording is a presentation and subsequent Q&A session by Mark Pesce, a media and technology commentator and former Apple engineer, addressed to staff at Lonely Planet. Speaking from his position at the National Film School, Pesce discusses the impact of digital distribution on the travel publishing industry. He begins by outlining his background in virtual reality and 3D earth modeling, noting his invention of the VRML protocol in 1994 and his work on early web-based earth models. He contrasts these early efforts with the recent release of Google Earth, arguing that it has established the world as a database and positioned Google as a potential threat to traditional publishers by aggregating geographically contextual information. Pesce emphasizes the power of "swarms" and user-generated content, citing Wikipedia’s rise against the Encyclopedia Britannica and Craigslist’s disruption of classified advertising as key examples. He argues that traditional editorial models are being replaced by community-driven curation and social credit systems, similar to those found on eBay. He advises Lonely Planet to embrace syndication and open content sharing, suggesting that their brand value lies in curating a trusted community of expert authors and passionate travelers rather than hoarding content. He recommends leveraging social networks and mobile devices to create contextual experiences for travelers, noting that the mobile phone is the primary device for the target demographic. During the Q&A, participants discuss the balance between expert authorship and user-generated content, the economic viability of free content, and the strategic threat posed by Google. Pesce acknowledges the importance of deep, expert-written content but argues that the brand’s future depends on facilitating a global conversation about travel. He warns against a "walled garden" approach, advocating instead for a model where content is widely syndicated to build brand authority. The discussion concludes with a focus on the need for rapid, iterative development to keep pace with technological changes, urging the team to define Lonely Planet not just as a website, but as a community and organizing principle for travel information.
