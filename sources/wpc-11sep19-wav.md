# WPC 11Sep19.wav

## Summary

This recording captures the opening keynote address by Mark Pesce at the 12th World Plumbing Congress in Melbourne. Pesce, introduced as a futurist, writer, and co-inventor of VRML, frames his talk around the concept that the future is already present but unevenly distributed. He begins by analyzing the Kohler Connect smart faucet, a product showcased at the Consumer Electronics Show, to illustrate how plumbing is becoming integrated with the internet and artificial intelligence. He explains that while the hardware technology is not new, the connectivity allows the device to interact with voice assistants like Alexa, effectively connecting the faucet to a global network of computing resources and machine learning systems. Pesce highlights the security risks of such connected systems, noting that plumbing can be weaponized by hackers, and emphasizes that these technologies rely on machines learning from billions of mistakes to improve accuracy. The speaker then shifts to the impact of automation on project management and labor. He cites Takeoffs, a Brisbane-based startup, as an example of AI and human collaboration streamlining the creation of bills of materials and project plans. Using the Tesla Model 3 production line as a cautionary tale, Pesce argues that fully robotic assembly fails because robots lack the embodied knowledge required for fiddly tasks, necessitating a partnership where humans handle complex adjustments while machines manage repetitive data. He predicts that over the next thirty years, construction and plumbing will see the integration of machine vision and AI at every stage, from material scanning to real-time quality inspection, transforming the role of the plumber into that of a partner working alongside intelligent tools. Finally, Pesce discusses the future of workforce participation, arguing that the traditional apprenticeship model of plumbing is a strength that the broader business world will need to adopt. He predicts that by 2030, most organizations will shift from static job roles to continuous apprenticeships, where employees constantly upskill to keep pace with accelerating technological change. He contrasts this with current corporate practices, such as Telstra’s layoffs, and suggests that plumbers have a unique opportunity to teach other industries how to build adaptive, learning-based cultures. The talk concludes with a Q&A session where Pesce addresses concerns about broadband infrastructure, data privacy, and the limits of robotics, reinforcing the idea that the plumbing profession must double down on its culture of learning to remain competitive and safe in an increasingly connected world.
