## Summary

This recording is a lecture delivered by Mark Pesce to an audience of tertiary educators, likely at a university or professional conference in Sydney, Australia. The session is titled "Facing the Mission or One Another: Tertiary Education in the Age of AI." Pesce, a futurist, former engineer, and academic, begins by acknowledging the Gadigal people and outlining his four-decade career in technology, including his work on VRML and his roles at institutions such as the University of Sydney and the Australian Film, Television and Radio School. He frames the talk around the rapid transformation of work and education driven by the release of ChatGPT just 30 months prior. Pesce presents several recent "weak signals" indicating that AI is already displacing human labor, citing Intel’s decision to outsource its marketing department to Accenture and AI tools, a report from The Atlantic on the bursting of the computer science job bubble for entry-level graduates, and The Economist’s findings on high unemployment among recent university graduates. He argues that these trends suggest current tertiary education models are no longer fit for purpose. To illustrate the cognitive impact of AI assistance, he references a study showing reduced brain engagement when using ChatGPT compared to working independently, drawing a historical parallel to the introduction of calculators in schools. He introduces the concept of "resistance," defining it as the degree to which a task, role, or career can withstand automation. He suggests that sectors like marketing, public administration, and parts of law have low resistance, while embodied roles such as trades, medicine, and caring professions have high resistance because they require physical dexterity and nuanced human judgment that robots cannot yet replicate. In the final segment, Pesce advises educators to recalibrate their institutions to focus on high-resistance areas, including the trades, healthcare, and human-centric teaching and mentoring. He recommends shifting assessments toward oral and face-to-face formats to prevent AI cheating and to emphasize embodied knowledge, citing the University of South Australia’s successful implementation of oral examinations. He concludes by comparing the current AI disruption to the massive shift from agricultural to industrial labor in the early 20th century, asserting that while the transition is confronting, society has successfully navigated similar labor upheavals before. He urges tertiary educators to guide students toward careers that leverage uniquely human capabilities to ensure they remain employable in a rapidly changing economic landscape.
