# 1_DIA_MarkPesce (1).mp3

## Summary

This recording is a talk by Mark Pesce, a media and technology commentator, likely delivered at a conference in New Zealand given his references to Radio NZ, Te Papa, and data.gov.nz. The session begins with an analysis of social networks, where Pesce argues that while platforms like Facebook and Twitter expand the number of people we can interact with, they do not overcome the natural human limit of 150 close relationships. He distinguishes between generic social networks and highly tuned community-specific platforms, such as LinkedIn for professionals or the newly announced Science Beat for scientists, suggesting that the future of social media lies in these specialized, persistent connections rather than broad, generic feeds. Pesce then shifts to the implications of social media for organizations, particularly government agencies. He posits that information flows no longer respect organizational hierarchies or rules, creating internal pressure to connect across silos and external pressure to engage with self-organizing communities. He advises organizations to adopt clear guidelines for social media use, citing the Australian Broadcasting Corporation’s four rules as a model, and emphasizes that authenticity is key to navigating the current "chaos" of having high power but low wisdom. He compares this technological shift to the steam engine, noting that it has amplified innate social capabilities without a corresponding increase in social understanding. The final segment of the talk previews Pesce’s upcoming work on augmented reality and mobile devices. He envisions a future where smartphones, equipped with cameras, GPS, and network access, act as magnifying glasses that reveal hidden data layers. He provides a practical example of scanning a package of meat in a supermarket to access traceability data regarding animal health, nutritional content, and carbon footprint. Pesce argues that this technology will require government cooperation to release relevant datasets, as the utility of augmented reality depends on the integration of public and private data to help individuals make informed decisions about health and sustainability.
