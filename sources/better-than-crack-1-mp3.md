# Better-Than-Crack (1).mp3

## Summary

This recording is a lecture by Mark Pesce, an honorary associate with the Digital Culture Program, delivered to a student audience. The talk, titled "Better Than Crap," explores the psychological and cultural impact of mobile technology, arguing that devices have become so compelling that they override basic evolutionary drives, such as the instinct to care for children. Pesce opens with an anecdote about a mother nearly dropping her toddler while distracted by a phone call to illustrate this point. The speaker then traces the history of his own relationship with technology, starting in 1975 when, as a twelve-year-old in Rhode Island, he first interacted with a computer via a teletype. He describes entering a "flow state" while playing a Star Trek simulator, which defined his career path. He connects this early fascination to the concept of hypertext, citing Ted Nelson’s work and his own experiments with linked information systems in the 1980s. Pesce recounts his first encounter with the World Wide Web in July 1993, initially dismissing it until he realized its potential for linking diverse interests. He details how the web grew through a process of "seduction," where users were hooked by content that reflected their personal passions, leading to the rapid adoption of the technology in the mid-1990s. The lecture shifts to the mobile phone, which Pesce identifies as the current agent of this seduction. He contrasts the utility of mobiles in developing nations, such as fishermen in India using them to find better markets and mobile banking in Kenya, with their role in developed countries. He argues that the device has inserted itself into human ontology, reshaping social behaviors and creating a constant state of connectivity. Using the astrophysical concept of the Schwarzschild radius, he suggests society has passed a point of no return, where the allure of the device is inescapable. The talk concludes with a Q&A session where students discuss their experiences with constant connectivity and the difficulty of disconnecting, highlighting the tension between the benefits of being linked and the loss of immediate, physical awareness.
