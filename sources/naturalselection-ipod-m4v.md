## Summary

This recording is a lecture delivered by Mark Pesce, likely at a conference such as Mind States, given his reference to a previous talk there and the context of the audience. The presentation is structured in three distinct parts, beginning with a discussion on tuberculosis and the evolution of drug resistance. Pesce recounts flying into the United States on May 17 and observing news reports about Andrew Speaker, a young man diagnosed with extensively drug-resistant tuberculosis (XDR TB). He explains the biology of TB, noting that one-third of the global population is infected, and details how the bacterium’s slow growth and thick cell wall make it resistant to antibiotics. He argues that the emergence of XDR TB is a direct result of human medical intervention, where incomplete treatment courses selected for resistant strains, a process he frames as a failure of modern medicine rather than an individual’s fault. The second section shifts to the history of evolutionary biology, focusing on Alfred Russel Wallace. Pesce describes Wallace’s 1854 experience in Malaya, where a bout of malaria-induced delirium led him to conceive the theory of natural selection while observing the distinct biological line between the islands of Bali and Lombok. He contrasts Wallace’s lack of social prestige with Charles Darwin’s established status and wealth, explaining why Darwin’s publication of On the Origin of Species was necessary to overcome the selection pressures of Victorian society and the Church of England. Pesce posits that the idea of natural selection survived because it was well-adapted to the scientific environment, a self-consistent principle that has universal import. The final part applies the principle of natural selection to warfare, specifically the First World War and the contemporary conflict in Iraq. Pesce highlights the Gallipoli campaign as a defining moment in Australian national identity and describes WWI as an industrial-era conflict where outdated tactics like cavalry charges were eliminated by machine guns. He draws parallels to the insurgency in Iraq, arguing that the decentralized nature of the insurgents and their rapid adaptation via the internet make them highly resilient. He concludes by quoting Lao Tzu, suggesting that violence always rebounds upon itself, and asserts that the 21st century is becoming a "Taoist century" where these ancient philosophical principles are recognized as physical laws.
