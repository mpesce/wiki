# Do Garbos Dream of Rootic Bins.mp3

## Summary

This recording is a lecture or talk delivered by Mark Pesce, an Honorary Associate in the Digital Cultures Program at the University of Sydney, to an audience likely comprising interaction design students. Pesce, who has nearly forty years of experience in technology, discusses the practical limitations and future applications of autonomous systems, arguing that current marketing hype regarding self-driving cars is disconnected from the cognitive complexity of urban environments. He illustrates this point with a personal anecdote about navigating a taxi through the ongoing construction on George Street in Sydney, noting that even experienced humans struggle with the dynamic, high-density decision-making required in such settings. Pesce introduces the concept of "decision density," explaining that autonomous systems will first succeed in low-density environments such as highways and rural areas, while dense urban centers will remain resistant to automation for the foreseeable future. He predicts that autonomous trucking will be one of the first major commercial successes, particularly benefiting remote areas like Broome, Western Australia, by reducing logistics costs. He also discusses the potential for autonomous drones in medical evacuations and military applications, noting that these systems will likely operate in low-density regions to minimize noise and regulatory issues. The latter portion of the talk shifts focus to less conventional autonomous systems, specifically self-driving waste bins. Pesce uses this example to introduce a framework developed by his colleague Genevieve Bell at the Australian National University, known as the 3As: autonomy, agency, and assurance. He argues that designers must consider not just whether a system can act on its own, but what its goals are and how its safety can be guaranteed. He posits that these questions are more profound when applied to simple objects like bins, prompting designers to think about the behavioral logic and satisfaction states of autonomous devices. The talk concludes by emphasizing that interaction designers will need to master these concepts as autonomous elements become ubiquitous in everyday life.
