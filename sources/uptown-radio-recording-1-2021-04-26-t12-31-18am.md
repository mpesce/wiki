# uptown-radio_recording-1_2021-04-26--t12-31-18am--mpesce.wav

## Summary

This recording is an interview featuring Mark Pesce, identified as an Honorary Associate in Digital Cultures at the University of Sydney and co-inventor of VRML with Tony Parisi. The conversation begins with a brief technical discussion about remote recording software that allows for local file capture before uploading to Dropbox, ensuring reliability against streaming interruptions. Pesce then shifts to discussing the history of online communities, tracing a progression from VRML-based worlds in the mid-1990s to Second Life in the early 2000s, and finally to Decentraland as the third wave. He contrasts the centralized server architecture of Second Life with the decentralized, peer-to-peer nature of Decentraland, arguing that the latter offers greater resilience because data is distributed across users' computers rather than dependent on a single authoritative source. Pesce critiques the economic foundation of Decentraland, describing its MANA token as a "Tinkerbell operation" where value is created through collective belief rather than intrinsic utility. He argues that while the decentralized structure distributes power more evenly than centralized platforms like Facebook, it remains fundamentally capitalist and subject to speculative market forces, comparing the "Landrush" events to casino dynamics. The discussion expands to the broader implications of virtual and augmented reality, with Pesce noting that current virtual worlds struggle to capture the richness of human body language and social interaction. He predicts that augmented reality will be the dominant medium in the coming years, expecting major announcements from Apple and Facebook around 2024 and 2025. He advocates for a decentralized approach to reality co-creation, contrasting it with the corporate control models of existing tech giants. The interview concludes with a reflection on the longevity of digital art and the speculative nature of cryptocurrency. Pesce highlights the risk of digital obsolescence, noting that many artists can no longer access work created thirty years ago due to lost hardware and software. He references the novel Snow Crash as the origin of the term "metaverse" and warns that artistic depictions can sometimes hinder technological progress by fixing public perception. The speaker encourages creators to consider the future-proofing of their work and suggests that current virtual environments are learning experiences that will evolve as the technology matures.
