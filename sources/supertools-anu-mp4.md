# Supertools-ANU.mp4

## Summary

This recording is a keynote talk delivered by Mark Pesce, a technology commentator and former Apple engineer, likely at a conference hosted by Dr. Bell. Pesce begins by acknowledging the Ngunnawal people and frames his presentation as a personal exploration of his lifelong passion for technology. He argues that his career has been defined by the development of "super tools" that fundamentally reshape civilization. He identifies three existing super tools: the spreadsheet, which he traces back to Dan Bricklin’s VisiCalc in 1979 and credits with enabling the personal computer revolution and late capitalism; the World Wide Web, which he describes as a networked hypertext system that amplifies collective intelligence; and the smartphone, which he notes became ubiquitous in just twelve years by detaching the web from specific time and space. Pesce then predicts three emerging super tools that will form the next technological environment. The first is augmented reality, which he argues will free users from the smartphone by wrapping the display around the head, thereby allowing for the "rewriting of consensus reality." This device will reveal the "digital depth" of the physical world, a concept he illustrates with the example of a modern car engine that hides its complex data systems behind a seamless surface. The second emerging tool is the population of real space with data, a project he compares to the early web’s growth from a manageable index to an overwhelming expanse requiring search engines. The third super tool is a new form of intelligence management, likely involving artificial intelligence, designed to filter the blinding overabundance of data created by augmented reality. To illustrate the necessary partnership between human and machine intelligence, Pesce cites the game of Go, where human players paired with AlphaGo outperform the AI alone. He connects this symbiosis to the visions of Buckminster Fuller and Douglas Engelbart, who sought tools to help humanity manage global complexity. Pesce concludes by stating that these tools are essential for "seeing" the world clearly, arguing that humanity is too powerful to operate blindly and must build instruments of sight to ensure a sustainable civilization.
