# EGA.mkv

## Summary

This recording captures a talk and subsequent Q&A session by Mark Pesce at the Electronic Frontier Australia (EGA) conference. Pesce, a media and technology commentator, returns to the stage after a thirteen-year break to discuss his thesis that technology functions as a drug, specifically focusing on Facebook as a global cognitive bias amplifier. He opens by citing a May article in The Australian reporting that Facebook executives pitched advertisers on targeting teenagers as young as fourteen based on their emotional states, such as stress or anxiety. Pesce connects this to a 2014 study where Facebook manipulated the news feeds of 689,000 users to demonstrate emotional contagion without their consent. He argues that Facebook’s machine learning systems, driven by the need to increase user engagement for advertising revenue, create detailed simulations of user behavior. These systems exploit human narcissism and cognitive bias by curating feeds that confirm existing beliefs, effectively trapping users in personalized reality bubbles that erode the public sphere and mental health. Pesce extends this analysis to the emerging threat of augmented reality, which he describes as a technology of constant surveillance that will allow Facebook to curate physical reality itself. He warns that the techniques used by Facebook are now being applied across all connected systems, including political manipulation via Cambridge Analytica and the potential medicalization of psychedelic drugs under surveillance capitalism. In response to these dystopian trends, Pesce offers three pieces of advice for the audience: know your substance by maintaining a healthy paranoia about free digital services; know your source by critically evaluating the provenance of information and refusing to accept second-hand truths; and know yourself by practicing mindfulness regarding the emotional impact of social media usage. The session concludes with a Q&A where Pesce addresses questions about the balance between technological utility and harm, the possibility of regulating monopolies, and the role of commons-based thinking in creating alternative tools. He discusses the dangers of state control over media monopolies, citing China’s social credit system as a cautionary example, and suggests that younger generations are already moving toward private, decentralized networks. Pesce also provides practical security tips, such as using password managers, VPNs, and the Tor browser, while acknowledging that security is a moving target. He emphasizes that the issue is not just about specific companies but about a new tool in the human toolkit that requires careful negotiation and transparency to prevent the weaponization of influence.
