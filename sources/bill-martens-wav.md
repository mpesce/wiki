# Bill Martens.wav

## Summary

This recording is a conversational interview conducted by Mark Pesce with Dr. William Martens, a perceptual psychologist and audio engineer. The discussion covers Martens’ extensive career in spatial audio, beginning with his early work on head-related transfer functions (HRTFs) and his 1987 paper that simplified their computational cost. Martens explains how these functions allow humans to perceive the size and acoustics of a space, noting that this sensitivity is often underestimated. He details his time at Northwestern University, where he founded Oris Corporation to commercialize spatial sound technology, and his subsequent work at UCLA and with Creative Technologies, where he contributed to the 3D sound API for the EMU8000 chip in the Sound Blaster AWE32. The conversation shifts to Martens’ involvement with VRML in the mid-1990s, where he helped define sound nodes for virtual environments, emphasizing that proximity should alter sound quality, not just volume. He also discusses a project with Thomas Dolby and Interval Research called Headspace, which involved capturing gestures to create interactive soundscapes over the internet. Martens then moves to his seven-year tenure at the University of Aizu in Japan, where he utilized high-density loudspeaker arrays to study spatial hearing without the need for head tracking. He highlights the importance of head roll for perceiving elevation and describes experiments at the University of Sydney using a 196-speaker array to simulate various acoustic environments, such as Gothic cathedrals. In the final segment, Martens addresses the future of spatial audio in augmented reality and data sonification. He argues that current virtual reality systems often cause discomfort due to sensory mismatch and lack of comprehensive multimodal integration, a concept he terms holoesthesia. He advocates for "hearables," or intelligent in-ear devices, that can filter noise while preserving important environmental sounds. He concludes by discussing the potential of off-head transducers in AR headsets to provide spatial navigation cues without occluding the user’s natural hearing, which is critical for accessibility and maintaining a sense of presence in virtual spaces.
