# ConnectedCity-2Aug11-H.264 for Video Podcasting.m4v

## Summary

This recording features a presentation by Mark Pesce, a media and technology commentator, discussing the concept of the "connected city" in the context of Sydney. Speaking in 2011, Pesce outlines how mobile technology has shifted urban life from physical connectivity to digital hyperconnectivity. He uses a personal anecdote about coordinating a dinner in Sydney via mobile phones to illustrate how residents now operate with an expectation of instant availability, contrasting this with the lower mobile ownership rates in Los Angeles. Pesce argues that this connectivity transforms the city into a platform for new social and economic activities, where information flows rapidly through personal networks rather than solely through official channels. He notes that this creates a situation where residents possess a level of situational awareness previously reserved for intelligence agencies, effectively making the city a vast, emergent knowledge network. The core of Pesce’s argument is that the city must adapt its governance to this new reality. He suggests that traditional top-down communication is insufficient because residents now share and curate information among themselves, often prioritizing peer networks over statutory authorities. To counter the potential for misinformation and social fragmentation, Pesce proposes that the city should facilitate the sharing of expertise among residents. He describes a future where every resident benefits from the collective knowledge of the community, leading to radical empowerment. However, he warns that this empowerment can lead to conflict if the city does not simultaneously enhance its own internal knowledge sharing and capability. He argues that city employees must become experts in the whole of the city to manage an increasingly capable and assertive populace. Following Pesce’s talk, a city official responds, acknowledging the challenges of managing viral networks and resident lobbying. The official discusses the importance of transparency and integrity when dealing with empowered citizens, noting that organizations are currently unprepared for the direct, digital feedback loops emerging in urban governance. A subsequent speaker highlights a pilot project involving people with disabilities using mobile applications to report infrastructure issues. This speaker emphasizes that while the technology exists, the organizational culture and customer service protocols are not yet equipped to handle direct, transparent communication with empowered users, creating a gap between digital capability and administrative readiness.
