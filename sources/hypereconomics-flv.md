# hypereconomics.flv

## Summary

This recording captures a public lecture and Q&A session delivered by Mark Pesce, a media commentator, writer, and former Apple engineer, at an event organized by the ITMP or MBITM program. The session, which appears to have taken place in late 2011, was chaired by a program representative who introduced Pesce as an inventor of VRML, author, educator, and broadcaster. The main topic of the talk is the economic and social transformation driven by hyper-connectivity, a concept Pesce explores in his book The Next Billion Seconds. The event included a brief mention of the recent passing of Steve Jobs, which the chair noted as a poignant coincidence given the focus on the future of technology. Pesce argues that the period between 1995 and 2026 represents a pivotal era in human history, comparable to the invention of language, because pervasive connectivity is eliminating market frictions. He illustrates this thesis with examples from the developing world, such as fishermen in Kerala using mobile phones to find the best prices for their catch, and farmers in Kenya using SMS services to access market data. He extends this analysis to Western economies, citing Australian retailer Kogan, Uber, Airbnb, and Zarly as businesses that aggregate supply and demand to create frictionless markets. Pesce predicts that these centralized platforms will eventually be disrupted by a decentralized economy where individuals and businesses interact directly through APIs, allowing for the assembly of custom value chains. The lecture concludes with a discussion on the implications for labor, currency, and government. Pesce suggests that traditional employment will be replaced by gig work, and that fiat currencies may be supplemented or replaced by virtual currencies like Bitcoin to facilitate global trade without territorial restrictions. In the subsequent Q&A, Pesce addresses concerns about government regulation, the fate of corporate hierarchies, and the potential for increased inequality. He maintains that the shift toward a networked, tribal structure is an inevitable result of connectivity rather than a revolutionary choice, and that while competition will remain, cooperation will also increase. The event ended with the chair thanking Pesce and directing attendees to a local pub for networking.
