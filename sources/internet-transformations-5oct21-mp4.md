# Internet Transformations 5Oct21.mp4

## Summary

This recording is a lecture delivered by Mark Pesce, a media and technology commentator and former engineer, likely at an academic or professional conference. The talk explores the history, social impact, and ethical implications of augmented reality (AR) and virtual reality (VR). Pesce begins by recounting his 1994 work with Tony Parisi on VRML, the first 3D interface for the World Wide Web, which was later superseded by WebGL. He then transitions to the social dynamics of AR, using the 2016 launch of Pokemon Go in Australia as a primary case study. He describes how the game’s placement of digital assets in physical spaces, such as Peg Patterson Park in Rhodes, Sydney, led to a massive gathering of players, illustrating his thesis that inscribing data into space fundamentally changes human behavior in that location. Pesce argues that AR is not merely a technological apparatus like head-mounted displays, but a human activity defined by binding data to specific places, a practice dating back to cave paintings. He highlights the concept of the "right to write," questioning who has the authority to place digital content in physical spaces and the potential for harassment or corporate control. The lecture also addresses privacy concerns, noting that AR devices require detailed spatial awareness and gaze tracking, creating a form of networked surveillance. He references Facebook’s Project Aria and the broader trend of data collection, warning that these systems could function as a panopticon, revealing private behavioral data to corporations. In the final segment, Pesce discusses the economic and environmental benefits of AR transparency. He uses the example of modern automobiles, where manufacturers obscure engine data to maintain control over maintenance, arguing that AR could empower users with the information needed to repair devices and manage energy consumption. He concludes by showing a 1995 video of the TVision project, a virtual globe system that was more ambitious than today’s Google Earth, to emphasize the need for AR tools that provide deep, useful data rather than just marketing surfaces. The talk serves as a call for careful consideration of data ownership, privacy, and the societal impact of spatial computing.
