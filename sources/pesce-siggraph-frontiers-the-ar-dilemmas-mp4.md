# Pesce - SIGGRAPH FRONTIERS - The AR Dilemmas.mp4

## Summary

Mark Pesce of the University of Sydney presents a SIGGRAPH Frontiers talk titled "The AR Dilemmas," outlining the historical and ethical complexities of augmented reality. Pesce, a co-creator of VRML in 1994, argues that AR is fundamentally the binding of metadata to a location and the rendering of that data, a concept that predates modern computer graphics. He traces the lineage of AR to Ivan Sutherland’s 1965 "ultimate display" paper and the 1968 head-mounted display, noting that real-time 3D graphics were invented specifically to support this vision. Pesce contends that the current association of AR with computer graphics is a historical artifact rather than a technical necessity, as located metadata can be rendered through various means, including text. The speaker uses the 2016 release of Pokémon Go in Sydney to illustrate the social impact of located metadata. He describes how a single PokéStop in Peg Patterson Park triggered a feedback loop that resulted in a crowd of one thousand people, leading to noise complaints and police intervention. This incident demonstrates that altering space changes human behavior, a principle often ignored by tech companies. Pesce critiques the naivety of early AR art installations, such as a Facebook headquarters piece, and highlights the emerging "right to write" in physical space. He argues that current commercial systems from Microsoft, Google, and Apple treat space as a writable canvas without respecting private property boundaries, a stance that will likely face legal challenges. Pesce further identifies AR as a technology of networked surveillance, describing devices like Facebook’s Project Aria as "omnidirectional panopticons." These systems capture outward environmental data and inward biometric data, particularly through gaze tracking, which reveals user intent and attention. He warns that these devices will stream vast amounts of data to cloud services to create user profiles and increase engagement, raising significant privacy concerns. The talk concludes with a call for thoughtful design that prioritizes human agency and reflects on the emergent, often unpredictable, social consequences of deploying AR systems at scale. Pesce references his book, "Augmented Reality Unboxing Texts Next Big Thing," for further details.
