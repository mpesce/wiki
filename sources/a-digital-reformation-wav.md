# A Digital Reformation.wav

## Summary

This recording is a keynote speech delivered by Mark Pesce in Toronto, introduced by an audience member named Anna. Pesce, a media and technology commentator and former Apple and Mozilla engineer, begins by acknowledging the traditional lands of the Mississauga of the First Credit. He frames his talk around the 500th anniversary of Martin Luther’s 95 Theses, drawing a parallel between the Protestant Reformation and the need for a "digital reformation" against what he terms digital colonialism. Pesce argues that the open web has been colonized by digital monopolies, primarily Facebook, which transformed public digital space into a closed, surveilled environment to maximize user engagement for advertisers. He explains that Facebook’s algorithmic curation amplifies cognitive biases by feeding users content that reinforces their existing worldviews. The speaker then shifts to augmented reality (AR), noting that while AR technology has advanced significantly since Ivan Sutherland’s early work, it is fundamentally a technology of surveillance. He cites Pokemon Go as an example of AR treating the physical world as a plaything, leading to issues like nuisance gatherings and inappropriate usage at sensitive sites like Auschwitz. Pesce highlights that new hardware, such as Apple’s TrueDepth camera and Google’s Project Tango, enables simultaneous localization and mapping, allowing devices to track location and facial expressions in real time. He warns that the convergence of Facebook’s profiling algorithms, smartphone surveillance hardware, and AR interfaces will create a "Pollyanna machine." This system will provide a fully curated, bias-reflecting view of reality, making the unfiltered world uncomfortable by comparison. To counter this, Pesce proposes three principles: judging technologies by their social good and harm, ensuring private value accrues only after social costs are accounted for, and rejecting the "ask forgiveness, not permission" mindset in favor of seeking permission. He references his own research on the Mixed Reality Service as a framework for managing permissions in AR. The speech concludes with a call to action for a digital reformation, ending with the quote from Martin Luther, "I can do no other."
