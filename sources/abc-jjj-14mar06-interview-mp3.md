# ABC-JJJ-14Mar06-Interview.mp3

## Summary

This recording is an interview segment from Australia's National Youth Radio Station, Triple J, featuring media theorist Mark Pesce. The discussion centers on the impact of BitTorrent technology and digital distribution on the commercial television industry. Pesce explains that BitTorrent is a peer-to-peer software protocol that distributes files efficiently by allowing users to share copies with one another, reducing the bandwidth burden on central servers. He notes that this method has become the most effective way to distribute content, with 34 percent of all internet traffic attributed to BitTorrent. Consequently, almost all major Hollywood films and television broadcasts from the UK, Australia, and America are available through this network. Pesce highlights that Australians download more television content than any other demographic, partly because they receive shows later than their original broadcast regions. The conversation shifts to the marketing implications of digital leaks, citing the example of a Doctor Who episode that leaked via BitTorrent in March of the previous year. Despite the unauthorized release, the episode generated significant buzz and achieved the highest ratings in the show's history when it officially aired, suggesting that early digital access can serve as a promotional tool. Pesce argues that traditional advertising models are under pressure, potentially forcing producers to rely on product placement or live event formats to maintain revenue. He posits that television, which began as a live medium, will likely return to that format to remain relevant. A primary concern raised by Pesce is not piracy itself, but the rise of amateur content creators. With the ease of digital distribution, audiences now have millions of content choices, allowing them to bypass professionally produced shows in favor of amateur works. This shift threatens both commercial and public broadcasters by fragmenting audience attention. The interview concludes with a comparison to the radio industry
