# Empire 6-11-06.mp3

## Summary

This recording is a keynote talk delivered by Mark Pesce, a media commentator and former Apple engineer, at a conference in Seattle. The speech, titled "Empire, Part One: Houses of Stone," is part of a three-part series and reflects on Pesce’s recent journey around the world. He opens by establishing his credibility and lack of commercial motive, noting that the talk is available under a Creative Commons license. The narrative begins with his arrival in Pisa, Italy, where, amidst jet lag and missing luggage, he experiences a profound epiphany in the Pisa Cathedral. He describes the ancient structure as a "human Wikipedia," where the layered history and architecture amplify rather than overwhelm the visitor, creating a sense of visceral participation in the culture. Pesce contrasts this quiet, ancient experience with his visit to St. Peter’s Basilica in Rome, which he describes as loud and thundering. He details the immense scale of the building, the Baroque bronze Baldacchino, and Michelangelo’s Pieta. He interprets the Pieta not just as a religious icon but as an archetype representing the endurance of the female and the mortality of the male, a theme he traces back to ancient myths. He argues that the Roman Empire never truly died but evolved, exchanging temporal power for spiritual authority, a continuity he feels in the heart of the Vatican. Returning to Pisa, he notes that the grandeur of Rome has drowned out the sacred stillness he previously felt, illustrating how grandeur can be potent yet silent. The talk then shifts to the American West, where Pesce drives from San Diego to Seattle. He describes his time in Humboldt County, California, as a return to a sensory, stoned childhood, before moving to the Oregon coast, which he finds exhilaratingly raw. In the Olympic Peninsula rainforest, he observes the stark contrast between the lush temperate rainforest and the "beautiful motherfucking clear cuts" of the national forest, using this as a metaphor for the lack of ecology in mass media. He connects this to a conversation with a friend who does not consume news, suggesting that stepping outside the culture of media saturation allows one to feel the "sickening yaw" of the craft.
