# NextWave.mp4

## Summary

This recording is a talk by Mark Pesce, a media and technology commentator, who discusses the rapid evolution of global connectivity and the emergence of a "human network." He begins by noting that in March of the current year, the 3.4 billionth mobile phone was sold, marking the moment when half the world’s population owned a mobile device. Pesce argues that this shift, which occurred over just ten years, has plugged billions of people into a system of instantaneous global reach, creating a world fundamentally different from previous generations. He structures his presentation around three examples of how this connectivity functions, describing the experience as both utopian and dystopian. The first example focuses on Twitter as a social messaging service that allows for real-time information sharing. Pesce details how Twitter users in China provided immediate reports of the Sichuan earthquake, identifying the epicenter and damage within minutes, long before traditional news outlets like the BBC or New York Times could dispatch reporters. He highlights the case of Dedrick Lam, a user in Shanghai who acted as a central clearinghouse for information, demonstrating how distributed networks can outpace traditional media in live reporting. The second example illustrates the potential for social media to be used as a weapon. Pesce recounts a story about a friend named Gregory, who was publicly humiliated by a managing director. Pesce explains that while Gregory had few Twitter connections, Pesce and another user, Raven Zachary, had large followings. They realized they could easily ruin the executive’s reputation and business prospects with a single tweet, a power Pesce describes as holding a "nuclear launch button." The final example examines the power of blogging to hold government officials accountable. Pesce describes how Josh Marshall, founder of the political blog Talking Points Memo, and his readership uncovered the unprecedented firing of eight U.S. attorneys in 2007. By crowd-sourcing the review of thousands of subpoenaed Department of Justice documents, Marshall’s community traced the firings to Attorney General Alberto Gonzales. This investigation led to a congressional inquiry and ultimately Gonzales’s resignation in August 2006.
