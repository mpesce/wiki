# Listen-25Oct11.mp3

## Summary

This recording is a talk delivered by Mark Pesce, a media and technology commentator, to an audience in Australia. Pesce notes that he has lived in the country for eight years, a period during which global mobile phone subscriptions reached six billion, making human connection synonymous with connectivity. He introduces his book, The Next Billion Seconds, which argues that the period from 1995 to 2026 will be as significant to humanity as the invention of language. The talk originated from a request by the Screen Producers Association of Australia to discuss film and television distribution during the early days of iTunes and BitTorrent. Pesce explains that traditional audiences have evolved into marketers, advertisers, and distributors, empowered by high smartphone ownership rates in Australia. This shift has created a world of media abundance where information flows without friction, leading to a transition from controlled channels to a chaotic, decentralized network of interest-based sharing. Pesce argues that this environment is not the end of marketing but requires a fundamental shift in strategy, summarized by the single word: listen. He cites the Cluetrain Manifesto’s assertion that markets are conversations and emphasizes that businesses must listen in real-time to public channels like Twitter. Listening allows companies to monitor brand sentiment, identify potential crises before they escalate, and gather product ideas from customers. He contrasts this with examples of corporate failure, such as a credit card company’s public meltdown on Twitter and Qantas’s delayed response to an engine failure, against positive examples like Telstra’s engagement with a customer. Pesce stresses that listening must be integrated into the business DNA, involving three dimensions: listening to the organization itself, to its customers, and to customers talking to each other. The speaker concludes by sharing a personal anecdote about being repeatedly asked for credit card upgrades by a bank that failed to remember his previous interactions, illustrating a failure of internal listening. He argues that while tools for data analysis exist, the real challenge is organizational culture. Pesce advises that listening is a gold mine that costs little but requires a complete redesign of business practices. He ends by urging organizations to consider how the imperative to listen should alter their entire production and operational processes, moving beyond traditional marketing tactics that prioritize talking over hearing.
