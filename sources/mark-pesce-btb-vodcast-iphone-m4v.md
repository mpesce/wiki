# mark pesce BTB vodcast iphone.m4v

## Summary

This recording is a lecture delivered by Mark Pesce to an audience of educators, likely in Australia, discussing the impact of network technology on pedagogy. Pesce begins by recounting his history with the education sector, noting that his involvement began around 2004 when he was the first panelist to mention Wikipedia to a group of Australian teachers. He frames the talk around three major cultural shifts: the birth of the web in the mid-1990s, the rise of interactive sharing platforms like Wikipedia after the dot-com collapse, and the explosion of social networks following Facebook’s 2007 platform launch. He argues that these shifts have created a pervasive "educational field" where information and social connections are ubiquitous, fundamentally altering the traditional classroom dynamic. Pesce contends that educators must act as moral guides in this new environment, as parents are often overwhelmed by the novelty of social media. He highlights the tension between the constructivist theory of learning, which emphasizes play and child-led exploration, and the rigid, industrial-style instruction model currently dominant in schools. He argues that the network provides the resources to support a more constructivist approach by connecting students with mentors and peers beyond the classroom walls. He references a conversation with Dr. Evan Arthur of Dior regarding the "unfinished constructivist project," suggesting that technology finally allows for the personalized mentorship that constructivism requires but which traditional school structures cannot afford. The speaker concludes by addressing Australia’s national curriculum, viewing it not as a static document but as the architecture for a new educational network. He proposes that the curriculum should be used to identify experts and create shared resources, effectively building an Australian Wikipedia for pedagogical purposes. Pesce warns against viewing the curriculum as a mandate for traditional top-down instruction, instead urging educators to use it as a framework to connect learners, mentors, and parents. He emphasizes that while the transition is complex, the goal is to marry the demands of standardized education with the playful, exploratory nature of constructivism, ensuring that every child benefits from a connected, high-quality education regardless of their background.
