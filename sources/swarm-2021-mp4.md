## Summary

This recording is an interview with Mark Pesce, a veteran of the virtual reality and web development communities, conducted by a host named Vanessa. Speaking from Sydney, Pesce begins by tracing the intellectual lineage of the metaverse through science fiction, citing Vernor Vinge’s 1981 novella True Names, William Gibson’s cyberspace, and Neal Stephenson’s 1993 novel Snow Crash. He argues that these works were not merely imaginative but reflected the technological realities of their times, with Vinge and Gibson commenting on the emerging capabilities of computing and corporate culture. Pesce distinguishes between the metaverse as a broad set of possibilities and the specific, economically driven instrumentality promoted by Facebook, which he critiques for prioritizing head-mounted displays that are uncomfortable and biologically limiting for many users, particularly women and older adults. Pesce advocates for a different purpose for virtual spaces, suggesting they should serve as tools for planetary management and community building rather than just paid labor environments. He highlights the technical challenges of representing human embodiment in virtual worlds, noting that current avatars often fail the uncanny valley test. He points to video grammetry and depth cameras as potential solutions for creating more realistic, volumetric representations that respect the physicality of human interaction. The conversation also touches on the history of online community management, referencing the 1993 International Conference on Cyberspace and the ethical dilemmas that arose from early text-based multiverses. Pesce reflects on his own experiences moderating the VRML mailing list in the early 1990s, emphasizing the resilience required to handle harassment and the evolving professionalization of content moderation in the wake of recent legal rulings. The discussion concludes with a focus on preservation and the future of augmented reality. Pesce shares the story of Daniel’s Story, an early VRML work he created with Tony Parisi, which was recently rediscovered on a server after being thought lost for decades. This anecdote underscores the fragility of digital archives and the difficulty of preserving complex, distributed network systems.
