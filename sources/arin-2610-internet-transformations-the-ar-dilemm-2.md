# ARIN 2610 Internet Transformations The AR Dilemmas 5Oct21 1080p.mp4

## Summary

This recording is a lecture delivered by Mark Pesce, a media and technology commentator and former Apple engineer, likely at a university or academic institution given the reference to assigned reading and a host named Terry. Pesce begins by recounting his history in virtual reality, including co-inventing the first 3D web interface, Labyrinth, with Tony Parisi in 1994, and its evolution into modern WebGL standards. He transitions to the topic of augmented reality (AR), using the 2016 release of Pokemon Go in Australia as a case study. He details how the game’s placement of digital assets in physical spaces, specifically at Peg Patterson Park in Rhodes, Sydney, triggered a massive social gathering that police had to disperse, illustrating how inscribing data into space fundamentally alters human behavior. Pesce argues that augmented reality is not merely a technological device like head-mounted displays but a human activity defined by binding data to specific locations, a practice dating back to ancient cave paintings. He critiques the current technological landscape, noting that AR devices inherently function as tools for networked surveillance. Citing Facebook’s Project Aria, he explains that these devices collect extensive data on user location, environment, and even eye gaze, creating what he terms a Disneyfied panopticon. He raises significant concerns regarding privacy, data ownership, and the potential for corporate control over public spaces, a concept he labels the right to write. He warns that without regulation, companies may assert the right to place arbitrary data in physical spaces, altering behavior and potentially infringing on property rights. Despite these risks, Pesce highlights the potential benefits of AR in providing transparency. He uses the example of modern automobiles, where manufacturers obscure engine data to maintain control over maintenance and repair, to argue that AR could empower users by revealing hidden metadata about devices and supply chains. He suggests that such transparency is essential for managing energy consumption and carbon emissions in a sustainable economy. The lecture concludes with a discussion of the 1995 TVision project, a virtual globe system that offered a more comprehensive view of global data than modern tools like Google Earth, encouraging designers to consider the depth and utility of data representation in future AR systems.
