# avatars96-keynotes-pesce-parisi_512kb.mp4

## Summary

This recording is a keynote address delivered at the Contact Consortium, featuring a speaker who introduces himself in the context of a presentation shared with Mark Pesce. The talk, structured in four parts, explores the evolution of the World Wide Web into a social, three-dimensional cyberspace, focusing on the technical standards of VRML and the sociological implications of digital avatars. The speaker begins by tracing the history of online communication from early wires to the Web, arguing that the lack of visual clarity and spatial orientation in 2D web pages created a necessity for a more immersive environment. He references the cultural impact of Neal Stephenson’s novel Snow Crash and the early virtual world Habitat to define the concept of the avatar as a human incarnation in virtual space. The second part of the talk addresses the development of VRML as an open architecture standard. The speaker recounts the "frontier days" of 1995, describing the community-driven effort to establish standards amidst competition from major corporations like Silicon Graphics and Microsoft. He explains that the formation of the VRML Architecture Group was necessary to move the technology from a chaotic, cowboy-style development phase to a stable, business-ready platform. He notes that while VRML initially faced criticism from gamers and Hollywood for lacking interactivity and visual flair, it ultimately provided the foundational infrastructure for animation, interactivity, and multimedia integration on the web. In the final sections, the speaker applies philosophical concepts from Michel Foucault to analyze the social dynamics of cyberspace. He introduces the idea of the "dossier," suggesting that digital interactions create a persistent record that can overshadow the individual’s direct experience, effectively turning the avatar into a prison of information. He concludes with the concept of the "panoptic self," drawing on Jeremy Bentham’s panopticon to describe cyberspace as an environment where observation is constant and identity is fluid. The talk ends with a call for the community to take ownership of the standards process, emphasizing that the consortium’s role is to empower developers and individuals to build the next generation of social virtual environments. The session transitions into a standards track focused on avatar protocols.
