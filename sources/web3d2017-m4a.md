# Web3D2017.m4a

## Summary

This recording is a conference talk delivered by Mark Pesce, a computer scientist and former Apple engineer, who opens by joking that he has missed the last fifteen iterations of this event. Pesce traces the history of computing from its philosophical roots in the work of Babbage, Turing, and von Neumann to the pivotal 1968 Fall Joint Computer Conference. He highlights two simultaneous breakthroughs that solved the problems of speed and complexity: Douglas Engelbart’s demonstration of the Online System, which introduced the mouse and hypertext-like linking, and Ivan Sutherland’s head-mounted display. Pesce argues that while Engelbart’s work was too complex for widespread adoption, the subsequent development of the World Wide Web by Tim Berners-Lee at CERN provided a simple, open solution that enabled the spread of interactive graphics. Pesce details his own career, including the founding of Ona Sendai Corporation in 1991 to create networked consumer virtual reality, and the co-creation of VRML with Tony DeRose. He explains how VRML evolved from a military and academic curiosity into a standard supported by major tech companies, eventually leading to the modern era of mobile VR, which he argues began not with expensive headsets but with the ubiquity of smartphones and simple cardboard viewers. He recounts a personal anecdote about successfully running a WebVR application on a modern Android-based television, demonstrating the maturity of the technology. The latter portion of the talk focuses on the social and ethical implications of mixed reality. Citing the release of Pokemon Go as a catalyst, Pesce argues that the immediate need for permissioning in augmented reality has finally given purpose to a protocol he developed decades ago. He describes the Mixed Reality Service, a W3C community group initiative he founded, which binds spatial coordinates to URIs to allow devices to query permissions in real-time. Pesce warns that without such permissioning systems, the fusion of the web’s knowledge with VR’s perception creates a potent tool for propaganda and confirmation bias. He concludes by urging the audience to participate in shaping the specification to ensure that the integration of virtual and real worlds remains safe and beneficial, rather than descending into an "endarkenment" of isolated filter bubbles.
