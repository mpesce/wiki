# Mark interviews Bill.wav

## Summary

This recording is a live talk and interview held at a media and technology studio, hosted by founder and CEO Chantal. The session features a conversation between Mark Pesce and Dr. William Martin, a perceptual psychologist and audio engineer. The discussion traces Martin’s career from his early work in the 1980s at Northwestern University, where he developed fundamental research on head-related transfer functions (HRTFs) and founded AORUS Corporation, to his later roles at UCLA, Creative Technologies, and the University of Aizu in Japan. Martin explains how HRTFs allow humans to perceive spatial information and environment size through auditory cues, noting that his 1987 paper provided an efficient method for processing this data, which was later commercialized by companies like Roland. The conversation shifts to Martin’s work in the mid-1990s with Intel’s RSX3D API and the development of VRML, where he contributed to sound features for virtual environments. He discusses the importance of head tracking for spatial audio, arguing that six degrees of freedom are necessary for accurate elevation perception, specifically noting that head roll, rather than pitch, disambiguates sounds above and below the listener. Martin also details his current research at the University of Sydney, where he utilizes a high-density loudspeaker array of 196 speakers to simulate acoustic environments without requiring head tracking. He highlights applications in architecture, such as predicting the acoustics of unbuilt spaces like spherical domes, and in music performance, where reverberation influences tempo and coherence. In the final segment, Martin addresses the future of spatial audio, emphasizing its potential to improve quality of life through natural communication in remote settings and its role in data sonification. He critiques the current state of multimedia where sound is often an afterthought, arguing that audio provides a deeper, more unconscious emotional impact than visuals. The talk concludes with a discussion on augmented reality, where Martin advocates for head-mounted devices with off-ear drivers, such as the Magic Leap One, to avoid sensory amputation for visually impaired users who rely on unoccluded hearing for navigation.
