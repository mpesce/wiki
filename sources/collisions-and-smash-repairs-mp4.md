# Collisions-and-Smash-Repairs.mp4

## Summary

This recording is a twenty-minute presentation by Mark Pesce, a media and technology commentator, delivered to an audience of educators, likely at TAFE Sydney given his specific references to the institution and local context. Pesce argues that the traditional balance of power in education has shifted from institutions to students, facilitated by new digital tools that allow for self-organization and peer review. He cites the website RateMyProfessors.com as a primary example, noting that it has become a definitive resource for students and a de facto tool for faculty hiring decisions, effectively wrenched control away from educators. Pesce posits that knowledge formation is no longer the exclusive domain of elites but is a multilateral process occurring from all directions simultaneously. The speaker outlines four significant technological revolutions that have occurred in the last four years, which he claims have fundamentally altered the educational landscape. These include the rise of YouTube as a comprehensive educational resource, the emergence of podcasting which allows for low-cost content distribution, the creation of Wikipedia as a new model for collaborative knowledge formation, and the mobility revolution enabled by devices like the iPhone. Pesce describes the resulting state as hyperconnectivity, where individuals are constantly reachable through multiple channels such as SMS, email, and instant messaging. He argues that this connectivity creates a tension between the isolated nature of the traditional classroom and the highly connected reality of student life outside its walls. Pesce concludes with four recommendations for educators and administrators. First, capture everything, as recorded lectures and discussions have enduring value and can be shared widely. Second, share everything, operating under the mantra that information is an economy of hyperabundance where sharing increases value. He cites universities like MIT, Stanford, and the University of Melbourne that publish lectures online. Third, open everything, advocating for open-source software like Moodle over proprietary systems like Blackboard or WebCT, which he argues are static and expensive. Finally, he urges institutions to embrace tools like Twitter to facilitate multilevel conversations and community engagement, criticizing the Department of Education and Training for banning the service. He ends by asserting that the classroom should be a permeable layer rather than a walled institution, leveraging the best tools in human history to foster collaboration and learning.
