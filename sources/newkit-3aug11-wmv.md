# NewKit-3Aug11.wmv

## Summary

This recording is a lecture delivered by Mark Pesce, who identifies himself as a digital ethnographer, to a classroom audience of approximately seventy students. The talk, which appears to take place in Australia around 2011 based on references to current events and technology, explores the cultural and economic impacts of mobile connectivity. Pesce begins by conducting a live survey of the audience to establish baseline data on age and the age at which individuals first acquired a mobile phone. He uses this data to illustrate the rapid decline in the age of mobile adoption, noting that the average age of first ownership has dropped from the late teens to the early teens over the last decade. Pesce argues that mobile phones have become a fundamental social lubricant and a "continuous lifeline," driving a cultural shift toward hyperconnectivity. He cites examples from India, Kenya, and Pakistan to demonstrate how mobile technology acts as an economic amplifier, allowing individuals to bypass traditional infrastructure and improve their livelihoods through information sharing. The speaker discusses the psychological implications of this connectivity, referencing anthropologists Robin Dunbar and Sherry Turkle to explain how humans maintain a cognitive limit of about 150 meaningful relationships, which serves as the foundation for social sharing. He posits that the innate human drive to share information is now amplified by digital networks, leading to viral content and new forms of situational awareness. The lecture concludes with a discussion on the "interest graph" and the potential for technology to reveal hidden expertise within social networks. Pesce describes a thought experiment involving a device that provides real-time environmental data to help a person survive on an alien planet, using it to illustrate how connected individuals leverage collective knowledge to make better decisions. He warns that this era of omniscience makes it difficult to keep secrets, citing instances where police operations were compromised by social media. The talk ends with an interactive exercise where students write down their areas of expertise on index cards, simulating a system that could match individuals with others who share or need their specific knowledge.
