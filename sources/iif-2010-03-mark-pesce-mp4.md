# iif-2010-03-Mark_Pesce.mp4

## Summary

This recording captures a lecture by Mark Pesce, a media and technology commentator and inventor of VRML, regarding the evolution of hypertext and its impact on reading habits and publishing. Pesce, introduced as a futurist and former chair of the emerging media program at AFTRS, argues that the networked nature of the web has fundamentally altered how humans process information. He posits that hypertext exerts a "centrifugal force" that pulls readers away from linear texts toward linked content, a dynamic driven by the inherent nonlinearity of the medium. Pesce contrasts commercial news sites, which minimize links to retain monetized attention, with Wikipedia, which embraces connectivity. He explains that this tension has led to the "TL;DR" phenomenon, where readers avoid long-form content due to the opportunity cost of distraction, resulting in shorter, more digestible texts rather than a genuine shortening of attention spans. He distinguishes between "publishing in light," which is merely a digital facsimile of print, and true electronic books that leverage the medium's capabilities. Pesce predicts that over the next decade, traditional books will dissolve into a dense, interconnected web of text, a transition he compares to the Schwarzschild radius of a black hole, where the discrete nature of the book disappears into a unified mass of human expression. In the final segment, Pesce examines how narrative fiction might survive this shift. He suggests that while standard page-turners may struggle against the lure of the web, works that create immersive universes, such as J.R.R. Tolkien's Lord of the Rings, or those that embrace multiplicity, like James Joyce's Finnegans Wake, are well-suited to the hypertext era. He concludes that the future of literature lies in texts that acknowledge their interconnectedness, transforming reading into an exhilarating, non-linear experience where all of human culture is reconstituted into a single, hyper-connected entity.
