## Summary

This recording captures a keynote address by Mark Pesce, an inventor, author, and futurist, delivered at a conference focused on automation and humanity. Pesce, who is introduced as a panelist on the ABC Human Letters Program and host of the Next Billion Seconds podcast, begins by defining his role as a futurist who analyzes trends to predict long-term business climates rather than predicting specific future events. He opens his discussion with the example of underwater infrastructure maintenance, contrasting the dangerous manual work of scuba divers with the emerging use of robotic inspection. He highlights the Open ROV project as a prime example of a twenty-year "overnight success" driven by the maturation of open-source technology, noting that open-source principles, similar to those powering the World Wide Web and Wikipedia, allow communities to collaboratively build and improve complex tools like submersible robots. Pesce then transitions to the topic of artificial intelligence, using the story of AlphaGo defeating the world champion at the board game Go to illustrate how machines learn from mistakes. He argues that this learning capability allows for a productive partnership between humans and machines, citing the startup Abyss Solutions, which uses AI to filter boring data from underwater drone footage so human operators can focus on critical issues. He contends that this combination amplifies human capacity, making work safer, faster, and cheaper, rather than simply replacing jobs. However, he raises significant ethical dilemmas regarding data privacy, using the example of water usage sensors that could reveal intimate details of a resident's daily life. He warns that without transparency and ethical responsibility, such data can be misused for profiling or exploitation, creating a "duty of care" for organizations. The talk concludes with a broader philosophical argument about the dangers of confirmation bias in a data-rich age. Pesce asserts that it is easy to build machine learning systems that amplify human paranoia or ignorance by only surfacing data that confirms existing beliefs. He emphasizes the critical need for "listening" to the data and to diverse human voices, rather than shouting over them.
