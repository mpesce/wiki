# The No Normal CFO Series NZ 8Jun21.mp4

## Summary

This recording is a conference talk delivered by Mark Pesce, a technology commentator and former Apple and Mozilla engineer, following an introduction by James. The speaker begins by referencing a recent ten-day visit to Shanghai and the widespread adoption of QR codes, using them to link to his biography and a podcast series he is currently producing on the global chip shortage. He explains that the shortage stems from both underinvestment and a surge in demand for personal computers and webcams during the pandemic, a situation that will take years to resolve. The main topic of the presentation is the permanent shift in workplace dynamics caused by the pandemic, specifically addressing the long-standing economic puzzle of stagnant productivity growth despite massive IT investment. Pesce argues that the pandemic acted as a trigger that forced workers to fully utilize their existing digital tools, comparing the previous state to using stone knives and the new reality to using chainsaws. He posits that this change in understanding, not the tools themselves, is the critical factor. Consequently, the workforce is now dividing into three distinct segments: office workers, who make up about a third of the total; remote workers, comprising roughly a quarter; and a new class of hybrid workers, accounting for approximately 40 percent. Pesce details the specific challenges each group presents to management. Office workers face uncertainty due to the absence of colleagues, requiring managers to actively support their interpersonal relationships. Remote workers, who are often high performers, require paradoxically more face-to-face time with managers to maintain focus and work-life balance, leading to pushback against mandates to return to the office, as seen in recent examples from Apple and Washingtonian magazine. Hybrid workers, the largest and most fluid group, necessitate continuous negotiation between managers and employees regarding flexible policies. The talk concludes with the prediction that by the end of the decade, hybrid work could account for 70 percent of the workforce, fundamentally altering the purpose of the physical office from individual productivity to team connection and strategy.
