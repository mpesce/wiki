# EGA.mp3

## Summary

This recording is a talk and subsequent Q&A session by Mark Pesce at the Electronic Gaming Australia (EGA) conference, a venue he notes he has not visited in thirteen years. The presentation, titled "Technology as a Drug," examines the psychological and societal impacts of social media, specifically Facebook, framing the platform as an addictive substance that manipulates user behavior. Pesce begins by citing a 2018 report from The Australian, which revealed that Facebook executives pitched advertisers on the ability to target teenagers as young as fourteen when they were feeling stressed or defeated. He connects this to a 2014 study published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, which demonstrated that Facebook could manipulate the moods of 689,000 users by altering their news feeds without their consent. Pesce argues that Facebook’s business model relies on increasing user engagement through sophisticated machine learning profiles that act as "cognitive bias amplifiers." By curating feeds that confirm users' existing beliefs, the platform creates personalized realities that erode the public sphere and accelerate political polarization. He warns that this model of surveillance capitalism is expanding beyond social media into physical spaces, citing examples like Google’s sidewalk stations in New York that use facial recognition to track pedestrians. Looking forward, Pesce predicts that augmented reality glasses will allow Facebook to curate users' entire visual reality, effectively removing free will by blending surveillance with immersive technology. He notes that these techniques are already being applied to other systems, including the potential medicalization of psychedelic drugs, which could be tuned by algorithms to keep users compliant. In the concluding portion of the talk, Pesce offers three pieces of advice for navigating this landscape: know your substance by maintaining a healthy paranoia about digital tools; know your source by critically evaluating the provenance of information; and know yourself by practicing mindfulness regarding one’s relationship with social media. During the Q&A, he addresses questions about the potential for regulation, suggesting that while state control of media monopolies is dangerous, commons-based thinking and peer-to-peer networks offer alternatives. He also discusses the importance of transparency in technology design and the need for individuals to adopt basic security practices, such as using password managers, VPNs, and the Tor browser, to protect their privacy against pervasive surveillance.
