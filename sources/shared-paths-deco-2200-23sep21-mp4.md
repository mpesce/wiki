## Summary

Mark Pesce delivers a lecture to a student audience, likely at the Australian Film, Television and Radio School, discussing the intersection of anthropogenic climate change, social inequality, and public health. Pesce, who identifies himself as the co-inventor of VRML and a media commentator, frames the climate crisis as the central design challenge for the next century. He references the recent interim IPCC report, noting that the link between carbon dioxide levels and temperature increases is now scientifically definitive. With atmospheric carbon currently at 415 parts per million, Pesce argues that current emission trajectories align with the apocalyptic SSP5 scenario, necessitating urgent action at the level of human habitation. The core of the presentation focuses on the work of Australian inventor Saul Griffith, specifically his upcoming book advocating for the electrification of all energy systems. Pesce explains that replacing diverse fossil fuel inputs with a single electricity source could reduce total energy requirements for the US and Australian economies to 42 percent of current levels. This shift transforms the home from a mere consumer of energy into a producer, leveraging Australia’s high adoption of solar photovoltaic panels. The lecture introduces the concept of the microgrid, where homes, electric vehicles, and batteries collaborate to generate, store, and share power within a neighborhood. Pesce highlights the design challenges of managing these systems, such as arbitrating appliance usage to align with solar generation peaks and negotiating energy sharing between neighbors. He emphasizes that these systems must be designed to be seamless and intuitive, akin to joining a Wi-Fi network, to ensure widespread adoption. Pesce concludes by reflecting on his own generation’s responsibility, noting that atmospheric carbon has tripled since his birth. He urges students to view climate change not as a hopeless doom but as a solvable design problem that will define their careers. He asserts that natural security is the foundation of national security and encourages students to read Griffith’s book to understand the technical and social frameworks required to build a sustainable future. The session ends with an invitation for questions, reinforcing the idea that designers have the power to create solutions that benefit both communities and the planet.
