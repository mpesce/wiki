# LCA2011-Keynote.mov

## Summary

This recording is a keynote talk delivered by Mark Pesce, a veteran software engineer and media commentator, at a technology conference. The speaker opens by reflecting on his thirty-two-year career in computing, tracing his origins from a high school PDP-11 to his work in virtual reality and the early World Wide Web. He details his role in creating VRML, emphasizing that its success was due to open-sourcing the code, which led to the formation of a global community. Pesce argues that this experience taught him that shared resources increase in value, a philosophy he has applied to his subsequent work as an educator and author. The core of the presentation focuses on the concept of mimesis, or imitation, as a primary driver of human behavior, citing research by Nicholas Christakis on how smoking, obesity, and even divorce spread through social networks. Pesce warns that digital platforms like Facebook have weaponized this instinct by capturing users' social graphs within closed, proprietary systems. He describes the social graph as the most valuable personal data, noting that surrendering it to a closed source allows for manipulation of individual tastes and politics. To illustrate the fragility of digital freedom, he recounts the 2010 WikiLeaks incident, where the site was deplatformed by Amazon, PayPal, and DNS providers, demonstrating that the internet is not inherently free and can be censored by state-aligned corporate forces. In response to these threats, Pesce outlines a "design guide for anarchists" comprising four principles: distribute everything, ensure transport independence, enforce strong encryption, and maintain open source transparency. He argues that resilient systems must be distributed and loosely coupled to survive both natural disasters and political suppression. He introduces his own open-source project, Plexus, a protocol designed to make social graphs portable and interoperable across different services, thereby breaking the lock-in of major platforms. The talk concludes with a vision for the "next billion seconds," predicting that mobile technology will further blur the boundaries between humans and machines, urging the audience to build open, resilient infrastructure to preserve individual liberty and privacy in this emerging transhuman era.
