# Wisdom -- Douglas Rushkoff Cuts Us Some Slack February 2021 1080p.mp4

## Summary

This recording is an interview conducted by Mark Pesce as part of a series he calls wisdom talks, celebrating the sixtieth birthday of media theorist Douglas Rushkoff. The conversation begins with Pesce framing the milestone of turning sixty as a time of accumulated wisdom and freedom, noting that Rushkoff has clearly articulated the challenges of the modern digital world through his numerous books. Rushkoff traces his intellectual journey from his first published work, Free Rides, to his seminal book Siberia, which explored cognitive freedom and the emerging potential of digital hypertext, fractals, and psychedelic culture in the early 1990s. He discusses how the early internet promised infinite play and creative novelty but was quickly co-opted by speculative capitalism, which sought to narrow possibility and program human behavior for market efficiency. Throughout the discussion, Rushkoff argues that the financialization and quantification of all aspects of life have stripped reality of its texture and relational value. He contrasts transactional entities, where machines excel, with relational entities, where humans thrive, suggesting that society has misplaced money and technology by using them to control rather than connect. He critiques the modern conflation of wealth with virtue, describing the cognitive dissonance of wealthy individuals who extract value through technology while shielding their families from its effects. Rushkoff also addresses the rise of conspiracy theories like QAnon, viewing them not merely as delusions but as organic, albeit dangerous, reactions to a technocratic system that denies people dignity and meaning. In the final segment, Rushkoff reflects on his personal wisdom, emphasizing the importance of giving and receiving slack from others in an era of persistent digital memory. He concludes by describing his current creative focus on community theater, specifically medieval mystery plays and Cervantes’ Entremeses, which he views as vehicles for exploring power dynamics and hidden narratives. He advocates for a WPA-style approach to theater that is accessible and open-source, believing that the act of participating in theater is more vital than professional performance. This return to theater represents a full circle in his career, bringing the lessons learned from the rise and fall of the digital internet back to the human, relational stage.
