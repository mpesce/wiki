# alcove-interview-23Jun08.m4v

## Summary

This recording is an interview from the podcast The Alcove, hosted by Mark Malero, featuring guest Mark Pesce. Pesce, identified as a technology futurist, writer, and pioneer of virtual reality, is in New York speaking at the Personal Democracy Forum. The conversation centers on Pesce’s self-identification as a "technology presentist," a term he uses to describe his focus on analyzing current technological behaviors and their distribution rather than speculating on distant futures. He argues that the distinction between Web 1.0 and Web 2.0 is less about hardware and more about a cultural shift from static publishing to dynamic communication. Pesce introduces the concept of "hyper distribution," noting that what was a novel phenomenon in 2006 has become a baseline cultural expectation by 2008. He further discusses "hyper connectivity," explaining how digital tools have expanded human social networks beyond the biological limit known as Dunbar’s number. He illustrates the dual nature of this connectivity with examples of both social unrest, such as the Cronulla riots in Sydney, and positive civic engagement, like the Xiamen protests in China that successfully halted a polluting plant. The interview also covers the political implications of these networks, specifically analyzing the Obama campaign’s use of social networks and the potential for these activated communities to persist beyond the election. In the final segment, Pesce addresses the challenges of information overload and the necessity for individuals to develop skills in managing attention and carving out quiet spaces for thought. He dismisses concerns about corporate censorship or net neutrality, citing Gilmore’s Law to argue that the human network naturally routes around artificial blocks. He concludes with an optimistic outlook on the next decade, highlighting the rapid global adoption of mobile phones, particularly in China, India, and Africa. He cites the Sichuan earthquake as an example of how hyper connectivity can provide real-time information and aid coordination, suggesting that these dynamic, often chaotic, human networks will define the social landscape of the 21st century.
