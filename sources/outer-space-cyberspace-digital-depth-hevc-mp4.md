# Outer Space Cyberspace Digital Depth HEVC.mp4

## Summary

This recording is a lecture by Mark Pesce, a media and technology commentator, delivered on the theme of space travel. The talk weaves together the history of computer interaction, virtual reality, and augmented reality with modern developments in spacecraft design. Pesce references his upcoming book, Augmented Reality, which is set for release in November of the year the talk was given, likely 2020 based on contextual clues regarding recent product announcements. The speaker begins by tracing the origins of interactive computing to Ivan Sutherland’s 1963 Sketchpad and his 1968 head-mounted display, which Pesce identifies as the first augmented reality system. He notes that augmented reality was invented before virtual reality but was more difficult to realize due to technological limitations. The narrative then shifts to NASA’s development of virtual environment workstations in the mid-1980s for astronaut training, explaining how these systems allowed crews to rehearse missions to embed procedures in proprioception. Pesce contrasts the analog, button-heavy cockpit of the Soyuz spacecraft with the minimalist, tablet-like interface of SpaceX’s Dragon capsule. He argues that this trend toward hiding physical complexity behind software interfaces, similar to the sealed engine bays of modern cars, reduces system resilience and repairability for end-users. Pesce proposes augmented reality, specifically through devices like the Microsoft HoloLens, as a solution to this design tension. He suggests that AR can provide "digital depth," revealing hidden data and affordances to users who need to diagnose or repair complex systems. The lecture concludes with a case study of Pokemon Go in Sydney, Australia, filmed on July 23, 2016. Pesce uses the incident, where crowds gathered in a park at night to catch virtual creatures, to illustrate how inscribing data into physical space fundamentally alters human behavior. He warns designers that as AR devices from companies like Facebook and Apple become common, their role will be to shape behavior in space by organizing and presenting invisible data, requiring careful consideration of the social consequences of such design choices.
