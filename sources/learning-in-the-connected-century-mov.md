# Learning in the Connected Century.mov

## Summary

This recording is a lecture titled Learning in the Connected Century, delivered by Mark Pesce to an audience of educators. Pesce, who notes his background in IT, discusses the relevance of technology in modern curricula, referencing a recent federal government decision to prioritize English language skills over IT training. He begins by describing his experience as a judge at the Young ICT Explorers event in New South Wales, where approximately 200 students from 28 schools presented their projects. He highlights a standout project by an 11-year-old student who built a custom LED display using a Raspberry Pi to manage playground access at his school. The student, who learned the necessary skills by watching tutorials on Adafruit, won the top prize. Pesce uses this example to illustrate the power of self-directed learning and the maker movement, noting that other winning projects included a smart recycling bin and an automatic guinea pig cage door, both built using Arduino technology. The speaker traces the theoretical underpinnings of this learning style to the work of Jean Piaget and Seymour Papert, explaining the concepts of constructivism and learning by doing. He details the evolution of educational tools from the Logo programming language to Lego Mindstorms and finally to Scratch, a drag-and-drop coding environment created at MIT. Pesce argues that the recent shift of Scratch to a web-based platform has enabled hypermimesis, a form of learning where students observe and imitate the work of a global community. This is supported by the availability of affordable hardware, such as the $35 Raspberry Pi and cheap tablets, which have narrowed the digital divide. He cites Moore’s Law to show how low-cost devices are becoming sufficiently powerful for educational use. Pesce concludes by discussing the role of institutions, such as libraries, in providing access to information and devices. He highlights the free release of Mathematica on the Raspberry Pi as a significant development for mathematics education, combining constructivist tools with community-driven learning. He asserts that the modern classroom is no longer a physical location but an attitude that marries learning by doing with learning from others.
