# TheNextBillionSeconds-ATP-25Feb12.wav

## Summary

This recording captures a keynote address delivered by Mark Pesce at the Heritage Film Festival, held at the Australia Technology Park. The event is part of a series titled "The Next Billion Seconds," which explores how technology will impact the world over the next 32 years. Pesce, introduced by host Roy as a futurist, author, and regular contributor to ABC media, discusses the profound societal shifts driven by mobile connectivity and the internet. He notes that the talk takes place in 2012, a time when mobile phones have become essential extensions of human identity, comparable to a phantom limb when lost. Pesce argues that mobile technology has transformed from a novelty into the most significant tool for wealth creation since the metal axe head. He illustrates this with examples from developing nations, such as fishermen in Kerala, farmers in Kenya, and barbers in Karachi, who use mobile phones to optimize their markets and increase their incomes. He highlights that by the end of 2012, there will be more mobile devices than people on Earth, leading to a state of hyperconnectivity where every individual is accessible to every other. This connectivity changes human behavior, as people now expect immediate communication and share their experiences constantly, a trait Pesce links to the evolutionary necessity of sharing information for survival. The speaker explains that social networks function not just as conduits for information but as filters that manage the overwhelming flow of data by allowing users to block noise and focus on relevant interests. He shares a personal anecdote about creating an encrypted Twitter tool, which led to his rapid integration into a community of cryptographers, demonstrating how sharing accelerates learning and expertise. Pesce concludes by noting that while this hyperconnectivity offers immense power and opportunities for collective action, as seen in the London riots and the Egyptian revolution, it also creates tensions around privacy and attention. He suggests that the current generation of sharing networks is merely a first step, and the future will see even more sophisticated ways for humans to collaborate and learn, fundamentally altering what it means to be human.
