# 1-02 The Poetry of Wallace Stevens.mp3

## Summary

This recording is a lecture from the third session of a course titled American Visionary Poetry, delivered by an instructor who identifies as a former Apple and Mozilla engineer. The speaker introduces the class by framing poetry as a vehicle for spiritual understanding in the modern era, specifically highlighting the work of Wallace Stevens alongside other American poets such as Whitman, Ginsberg, and Snyder. The instructor shares a personal anecdote about a mystical experience at a Zen temple while washing beets, describing a moment of expanded consciousness where the self perceived an infinite, lawful necessity in the flow of reality. This experience serves as the thematic backdrop for the lesson, which explores how poetry can address spiritual questions without relying on traditional religious or mystical practices. The lecture focuses primarily on three Wallace Stevens poems. The first is "A Primitive Like an Orb," recited in part by a student named Brooke. The instructor analyzes the poem as a reflection on the act of poetry itself, describing the "essential gold" of poetic expression as a way to capture ineffable spiritual moments. The second poem discussed is "The Idea of Order at Key West," which the instructor recites from memory. He explains that the poem addresses the "pathetic fallacy" by questioning whether nature possesses its own voice or if human imagination and artifice are required to give it meaning. The instructor connects this theme to the experience of Burning Man, noting how human creativity and the "rage for order" manifest in the desert landscape. The final poem examined is "Of Mere Being," often considered Stevens' last work. The speaker discusses its themes of death and the "artifice of eternity," drawing a parallel to W.B. Yeats' poem "Sailing to Byzantium." The lecture concludes by exploring the ambiguity of the golden bird in the poem, questioning whether it represents a natural essence or a human-made automaton. The instructor suggests that while human artifice may not fully transcend death, it offers a powerful, if constructed, framework for understanding existence, leaving the question of whether art can truly overcome mortality open.
