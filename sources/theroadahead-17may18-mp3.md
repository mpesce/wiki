# TheRoadAhead_17May18.mp3

## Summary

This recording is a lecture delivered by Mark Pesce, an honorary adjunct in a media and technology program, focusing on the history of virtual reality and the emerging technology of videogrammetry. Pesce begins by tracing the origins of VR to the 1950s, explaining how the complexity of jet fighter instrumentation necessitated new methods for synthesizing information for pilots. He highlights DARPA’s role in funding early research, leading to Ivan Sutherland’s creation of Sketchpad in 1963 and his head-mounted display prototype in 1968, which established the foundational elements of both virtual and augmented reality. Pesce discusses the subsequent development of the Virtual Environment Workstation at NASA Ames in the late 1980s, which used data gloves and a $2 million supercomputer to allow astronauts to practice missions. He also covers the commercial failures of the era, including Nintendo’s Power Glove and his own startup’s collaboration with Sega on the Virtua VR, which was abandoned due to motion sickness issues. Pesce then shifts to his work in the early 1990s on VRML, a 3D interface for the World Wide Web, noting that while VR hardware was too expensive and underpowered for mainstream use, the steady improvement of computer hardware over two decades eventually made consumer VR viable by 2014. The core of the talk introduces videogrammetry, defined as photogrammetry plus a timeline, which captures 3D models of moving subjects frame by frame. He describes the work of Link Gaskin’s company, 8Eye, which uses a dome of 40 high-definition cameras to capture human performances, such as boxing, with high realism that avoids the uncanny valley. Pesce demonstrates this technology using the Holo app and a PS VR experience featuring violinist Joshua Bell, showing how videogrammetry creates immersive, real-time 3D environments. The lecture concludes with the rapid advancement of this technology from a computationally expensive process to a real-time broadcast tool.
