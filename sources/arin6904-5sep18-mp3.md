# ARIN6904 5Sep18.mp3

## Summary

This recording is a lecture delivered by Mark Pesce, a media and technology commentator, who states he has worked in virtual reality for 29 years. The talk traces the history of VR from its origins in the 1950s as a solution to the complexity of fighter cockpit controls, funded by DARPA. Pesce highlights Ivan Sutherland as the most important figure in the field, noting his 1963 invention of Sketchpad and his 1968 demonstration of the "Ultimate Display," which established core VR features like head tracking and stereoscopic vision. The lecture covers the late 1980s NASA system that defined modern VR components, including head-mounted displays, 3D sound, and data gloves, and discusses the commercial failures of the Nintendo Power Glove and Sega VirtuaVR in the early 1990s, attributing their demise to design flaws and motion sickness caused by low processing power. Pesce explains that the VR renaissance began in 2014 with Google Cardboard, which leveraged the power of smartphones to make VR accessible to hundreds of millions of people. He details the evolution of high-end systems like the Oculus, HTC Vive, and PlayStation VR, and discusses the emergence of 360-degree video and interactive storytelling as new media formats. The speaker emphasizes the importance of body-computer coupling, noting that motion sickness results from a conflict between visual perception and proprioception. He also introduces the concept of videogrammetry, which allows for the capture of real-world objects and people into VR, citing a project involving a hologram of Buzz Aldrin. The final section of the lecture focuses on augmented reality (AR) as the successor to the smartphone. Pesce describes the Microsoft HoloLens and Magic Leap as early examples of AR devices that use depth sensors and waveguide lenses to integrate virtual objects into the real world. He predicts that within three to five years, consumer AR glasses will become widely available, offering a seamless blend of digital information and physical reality. The talk concludes with the assertion that major technology companies are racing to develop AR because it provides a direct route to user attention, potentially replacing the smartphone as the primary interface for digital interaction.
