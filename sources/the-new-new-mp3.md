# The_New_New.mp3

## Summary

This recording is a talk by Mark Pesce titled "The New New," in which he discusses the societal implications of technology rather than specific hardware. Pesce introduces the concept of "new neurosis," arguing that blogs function as complex ecologies with a low threshold for anxiety, constantly detecting and amplifying changes. He illustrates this with the viral spread of outrage over a Cotton On t-shirt, which moved from the Mama Mia blog to email, Twitter, and finally the Sydney Morning Herald front page within hours. He posits that networks thrive on novelty, and human desire to share experiences drives platforms like RateMyProfessors.com, which destabilized university tenure systems by allowing students to rate lecturers and share information freely. Pesce argues that hyper-connectivity leads to hyper-intelligence and hyper-empowerment, eroding traditional institutions like universities and newspapers. He notes that Stanford University and others are now sharing courses online via iTunes University and YouTube, further corroding the structure of higher education. Addressing an audience of government staff, he describes them as the "shock troops" of this revolution, caught between internal pressures to lock down information and external pressures to share it immediately. He warns that resisting the natural flow of sharing only builds pressure, while embracing it creates communities that may act in ways the organization does not control. In the final section, Pesce discusses his upcoming book, "She," which explores these themes of sharing. He explains that his literary agent rejected the project, citing the publishing crisis caused by the very sharing he advocates. Consequently, he decided to create the book as a collaborative, open process rather than a traditional authored text. He describes this approach as a way to "eat his own cooking," demonstrating that the more something is shared, the more valuable it becomes. The talk concludes with the idea that sharing is a constructive, ongoing process that defines the current era, leaving many questions about authorship and responsibility open for evolution.
