# RN_Encounter_CyberReligion_22Aug10.mp3

## Summary

This recording is an episode of the Australian Broadcasting Corporation's radio program Encounter, hosted by Kerri Stewart. The episode explores the intersection of religion and the internet, examining how digital technology is altering religious traditions, community structures, and spiritual practices. The discussion features several experts, including sociologist Dr. Brenda Brasher, Christian theologian Jennifer Cobb, and Zen Buddhist teacher Mary Jackson. A significant portion of the program is dedicated to an interview with Mark Pesce, a pioneer in virtual reality and the inventor of VRML, who discusses his personal spiritual journey and his work creating sacred spaces in virtual environments. The program begins by noting that early internet users were often neopagans and individuals from new religious movements, while mainstream organized religions adopted the technology later. Jennifer Cobb discusses the theological implications of cyberspace, referencing the work of Teilhard de Chardin to argue that the digital realm represents a new layer of evolution where consciousness is the guiding force. She suggests that cyberspace requires a deep reverence for the responsibility humans have in shaping the future. The conversation also touches on the historical parallel between the printing press and the internet, noting that both technologies have empowered laypeople by making sacred texts widely available and challenging the authority of religious institutions. Mark Pesce shares his experience as a gay Christian who found his faith disorienting, leading him to embrace pagan and Wiccan systems of thought that emphasize the connection between the individual and the world. He describes how his work in virtual reality became a mirror for this spiritual practice, noting that early VR pioneers were often pagans who used the technology to create collaborative sacred spaces. The episode also features Reverend Mark Brown, an Anglican priest who established a virtual cathedral in the online world Second Life. Brown discusses the challenges of maintaining religious authority and jurisdiction in a borderless digital space, where traditional geographic boundaries no longer apply. He highlights the tension between the embodied nature of sacraments like communion and the disembodied nature of online interaction. The program concludes with perspectives from Mary Jackson on the globalization of Zen practice through online retreats and a broader discussion on how religious organizations must adapt to a decentralized, networked future to remain relevant.
