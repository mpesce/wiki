# PowersOfThen-9Apr13.m4v

## Summary

This recording is a guest lecture delivered by Mark Pesce to a university class, likely at the University of Sydney, during week five of a course on cyberworlds. The session, dated to 2013 based on references to current events and student surveys, features an introduction by the course instructor who highlights Pesce’s background as an inventor, writer, and co-author of VRML. Pesce’s talk, titled "The Next Billion Seconds," explores the economic and social shifts of a digitally networked society. He begins by introducing the concept of metric time, using a time machine metaphor to travel back 318,000 years to pre-human Australia, then forward through 30,000 years of indigenous fire agriculture and animistic worldviews, to 1982, and finally to the present day. Pesce argues that the transition from an animistic worldview, where objects possess interiority, to the modern era of "enframing" described by Martin Heidegger, stripped the world of spirit in favor of human-centric utility. He posits that the rise of smartphones and hyperconnectivity is reversing this trend. By connecting five billion people, society has entered an era of hyper-sharing and hyper-learning, where knowledge is distributed globally. He cites the case of David Cecil, a truck driver who learned computer hacking online, to illustrate how connectivity empowers individuals. Pesce also conducts a live survey of students regarding their age of first mobile phone ownership and device types, noting a shift from basic phones to smartphones between 2010 and 2013. Looking forward, Pesce predicts that by 2045, the "Internet of Things" will become ubiquitous, merging the physical and digital worlds. He suggests that this will lead to a re-enchantment of the material world, where objects regain a form of social interiority, effectively returning humanity to a state of pervasive animism. The lecture concludes with a Q&A session addressing concerns about censorship, the erosion of individuality, and the impact of constant connectivity on human attention and institutional structures.
