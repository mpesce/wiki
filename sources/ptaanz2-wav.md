# PTAANZ2.wav

## Summary

This recording is a keynote address delivered by Mark Pesce, a futurist and podcaster from the University of Sydney, at a public transport leadership seminar. The event, identified by the hashtag #membertheleaders22, features a session titled "Sustainability Conversation." Pesce, who notes he has not owned a car during his 19 years in Sydney, addresses an audience of emerging leaders in the public transport sector. He begins by contextualizing the current moment within a broader historical shift, arguing that the pandemic compressed a decade of change into ten weeks, effectively ending the industrial era that defined economic and urban structures since World War I. Pesce explains that the industrial era centralized economic activity in offices and factories, necessitating rigid public transport schedules and peak-hour congestion. In contrast, the post-industrial era is seeing a return of the home as the primary locus of economic activity, driven by remote work and the service sector. He cites data showing that while private vehicle usage into city centers has doubled, the weekday population of Sydney’s CBD has dropped to 52% of pre-pandemic levels, whereas weekend populations have increased. This shift suggests that CBDs are transforming from work hubs into leisure and social destinations, similar to urban theme parks. Consequently, the traditional purpose of public transport, which was to move synchronized workforces, is becoming obsolete. Pesce argues that transport systems must adapt to serve new patterns of mobility, including later hours for retail and hospitality workers and decentralized travel to local shared office spaces. A significant portion of the talk is dedicated to a correction regarding autonomous vehicles. Pesce retracts previous hype about self-driving cars, citing insights from robotics expert Dr. Ken Goldberg and recent industry cancellations of major autonomous vehicle projects. He argues that true autonomy requires artificial general intelligence to handle the unpredictability of real-world traffic, a capability that is not imminent. Instead, he suggests the near future will rely on micro-mobility and hybrid transport solutions. He concludes by urging leaders to abandon industrial-era assumptions and embrace the post-industrial reality, viewing the pandemic not as a crisis to be reversed, but as an opportunity to redesign public transport for a decentralized, home-based economy.
