## Summary

This recording is a segment from the radio program Coast to Coast AM, featuring an interview with Mark Pesce, a media and technology commentator. The host, Maryette Hartley, engages Pesce in a discussion regarding the societal and ethical implications of nanotechnology. The conversation begins with a listener question about whether nanotechnology could create an alternate reality containing a fantasy mate, leading Pesce to argue that the technology will blur the line between the virtual and the real world. He notes that while this transition may be disorienting for older generations accustomed to a static world, younger people are already adapting to the in-betweenness of digital and physical existence. The dialogue shifts to the potential for abuse, comparing nanotechnology to drug abuse because it could allow for the direct stimulation of brain centers. Pesce highlights that the basic tools for nanotechnology, such as the Scanning Tunneling Microscope developed by IBM, are becoming inexpensive enough for widespread use, raising concerns about "mad scientists" in home environments. He discusses the "grey goo" scenario, where self-replicating nanobots could consume the Earth, estimating a 72-hour timeline for global destruction. Pesce suggests that containment would likely require nuclear weapons, a grim prospect that underscores the need for "anti-nanite" software developed alongside the technology itself. Pesce outlines the current state of research, noting that major universities and tech firms like IBM, Hewlett Packard, and Intel are actively involved. He predicts that artificial blood, made from diamond spheres, will be available within five to seven years, while a true nanocomputer capable of massive speed increases is expected around 2012. The interview concludes with a debate on whether social evolution is keeping pace with technological advancement. Pesce expresses cautious optimism, believing that society is beginning to understand the responsibilities associated with powerful tools, though he acknowledges that the current global situation regarding violence and conflict is not ideal. He emphasizes the need for individual and social responsibility as humanity approaches these god-like powers of creation and destruction.
