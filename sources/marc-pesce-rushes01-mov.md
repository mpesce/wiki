## Summary

This recording is an interview with Mark Pesce, identified as an Honorary Associate in the Digital Cultures Program at the University of Sydney and a media commentator with nearly two decades of experience. The conversation, likely recorded in 2015 based on references to current events and future projections, explores the state of the media industry and its trajectory toward 2030. Pesce argues that screens will become synonymous with the physical world, creating a pervasive environment where content is produced by people for other people, often outside traditional economic structures. He advises aspiring screen professionals to focus on long-form narrative storytelling and world-building, citing the resurgence of serialized novels in China and the cultural impact of Game of Thrones as evidence that audiences crave immersive, evolving stories. Pesce discusses the specific opportunities for Australian storytelling, suggesting that the nation’s multicultural identity, situated at the intersection of the West and Asia, offers a unique wellspring of narratives that can appeal to both global markets. He emphasizes that young creators, who have grown up in this multicultural environment, are best positioned to tell these stories. Regarding production, he notes that the cost of technology has dropped, making storytelling capability more important than technical mastery in short films, which now serve as part of a broader cross-media ecosystem rather than standalone pieces. He also touches on the economic model of the future, describing an attention-based economy where creators monetize by commanding audience attention and translating media experiences into lived experiences, such as theme parks or interactive platforms. The interview also covers emerging platforms, particularly the smartphone, which Pesce views as an underutilized network device for collaborative storytelling. He contrasts this with traditional broadcast television and highlights the potential for integrating narrative content into gaming cultures, referencing the Lego Movie and Minecraft. The discussion briefly shifts to the Australian market, where Pesce expresses frustration with Netflix’s licensing practices and notes that Australia’s strong consumer spending power makes it a significant market. He concludes by describing the attention economy as the fundamental currency of 2030, where social networks act as filters and amplifiers, allowing ideas to spread at the speed of light through many-to-many connections.
