# BiosAndLogos.mp3

## Summary

This recording is a lecture or talk by Mark Pesce, likely delivered at the Alchemical Arts Conference, focusing on a resonant theory of history that synthesizes the work of Terence McKenna with modern scientific concepts. Pesce frames the discussion around three nested waves of emergence: the origin of life four billion years ago, the emergence of the human species and linguistic consciousness 150,000 years ago, and the rise of technological civilization 5,500 years ago. He argues that these waves are converging toward a singular point of maximum novelty, a concept he links to McKenna’s "eschaton" and the popularized idea of the "singularity." Pesce draws on John McFadden’s book Quantum Evolution to explain the improbable emergence of life through quantum superposition, and Stephen Wolfram’s A New Kind of Science to describe how complex systems emerge through iterative processes rather than simple formulas. He posits that human language, or the "logos," operates at a speed roughly ten million times faster than biological evolution, effectively driving human culture and alienating us from our natural state. Citing Richard Dawkins’ concept of the "meme," Pesce suggests that linguistic units now propagate faster than genes, hollowing out individual interiority as they optimize for transmission. He connects this acceleration to the history of communication technologies, from the telegraph to the internet, arguing that we are approaching a state where nanotechnology will allow us to manipulate the material world with linguistic precision, creating a reality akin to magic. The speaker concludes by positioning the psychedelic community as the authentic search for a middle path between fundamentalist retreat and blind technological optimism. He references McKenna’s final inscription on a copy of The Invisible Landscape, interpreting it as a guide to transmuting consciousness. The talk ends with a Q&A session addressing questions on free will, the nature of memes, and the potential of non-verbal communication. Pesce suggests that free will is an emergent property rather than a given, and discusses how children are developing new linguistic and cognitive frameworks to adapt to the rapid pace of modern information, viewing conditions like ADHD as potential adaptations to this environment rather than solely as disorders.
