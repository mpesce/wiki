# 2-01 The Future of Contemporary Art.mp3

## Summary

This recording is a talk by Mark Pesce, delivered as part of a program that also included segments on contemporary art, a Philip K. Dick story hour, and a discussion on the pathetic fallacy. Pesce describes his presentation as a reading rather than a memorized speech, using a gong to begin. He opens with a historical reference to the lost Roanoke colony and the concept of "Croatoan" as a metaphor for alternative communities. He then recounts his time in New York City, where he stayed in a loft housing a private collection of over 3,000 volumes on 20th-century art, including works by Duchamp and Dali. Pesce expresses a deep disillusionment with the New York art world, characterizing it as a "dead culture" dominated by irony, trust funds, and specialized jargon that alienates the general public. He contrasts this with his experience at Burning Man, which he views as a "Temporary Autonomous Zone" inspired by Hakim Bey’s writings. Pesce argues that Burning Man functions as a crucible for radical free expression and community building, where art is ephemeral and often burned at the end of the event. He highlights the importance of participation and the rejection of archival permanence, noting that the best playa art is tied to the specific context of the desert. He then shifts focus to contemporary Native American art, specifically discussing a work by an artist who juxtaposed Hopi Kachina figures with corporate logos like Chanel and MTV. This juxtaposition, Pesce argues, illustrates the hybrid reality of modern Native Americans who navigate both pre-conquest spiritual systems and US monoculture. He recounts a phone interview with Comanche photographer Walter Bigby, who challenged Pesce on his own cultural roots, leading Pesce to reflect on his Irish heritage and the loss of pre-Christian traditions. The talk concludes with Pesce’s stance on the relationship between Burning Man and the mainstream art world. He references large-scale playa art pieces like "Ukrainia" and David Best’s "Temple Project," suggesting that while they may possess artistic merit, they should not seek validation from institutions like the Whitney or the Venice Biennale.
