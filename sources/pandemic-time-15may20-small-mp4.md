# Pandemic Time 15May20 small.mp4

## Summary

This recording is a lecture delivered by Mark Pesce, a futurist and former Apple and Mozilla engineer, to an audience at the Australian National University, likely associated with the 3AI initiative. Speaking via Zoom on his first day teaching remotely, Pesce acknowledges the Gadigal people of the Eora Nation before pivoting from his original planned topic on health quantification to a discussion on "pandemic time." He defines his role as a futurist as helping people understand the timescales they operate in to make reasonable decisions, arguing that the current global crisis requires a new framework for understanding temporal density. Pesce introduces the concept of "dense time," a period where significant events occur at such a high frequency that the forward planning horizon contracts drastically. He identifies the peak of this phenomenon in the United States around March 11, 2020, and in Australia around March 12, 2020, marking the moment when the future became effectively unknowable. Using the metaphor of an exponential curve where the horizon bends upward into a wall, he explains that human cognitive systems, which rely on predicting the future based on past events, fail during these periods. At the height of the pandemic, Pesce notes that the reliable forward planning horizon for individuals shrank to approximately 30 minutes, triggering survival instincts and behaviors such as panic buying and lockdown adherence. As the curve flattened in Australia, Pesce describes the gradual expansion of the planning horizon from 30 minutes to 30 hours, and eventually to several weeks, allowing for a sense of normalcy and boredom. However, he warns that dense time is not a one-time event but a recurring phenomenon that will likely happen again in different forms. He emphasizes the need for a "short now foundation," a counterpoint to the Long Now Foundation, to develop skills for navigating radically foreshortened time. The lecture concludes with a focus on empathy, noting that while Australia is exiting the acute phase, other nations like the United States remain in dense time, creating a global landscape of multiple, conflicting timescales that requires new approaches to engagement and support.
