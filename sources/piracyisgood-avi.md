# PiracyIsGood.avi

## Summary

This recording is a talk delivered by Mark Pesce, a media and technology commentator, likely at an Australian media industry event in late 2004. The presentation, titled "Piracy is Good," argues that file-sharing technologies like BitTorrent are not threats to the television industry but opportunities to be harnessed for economic gain. Pesce begins by explaining how BitTorrent works, noting that unlike traditional server-based distribution, its efficiency increases with popularity, creating a "swarm" where more users make the content easier to access. He cites the 2004 premiere of the new Battlestar Galactica series as a key example, where the UK broadcast on Sky One was quickly downloaded by US fans via BitTorrent. Contrary to expectations, this piracy did not hurt ratings; instead, it generated buzz that led to record viewership on the Sci-Fi Channel when it aired in the US. He similarly notes that a leak of the new Doctor Who series boosted its ratings in the UK. Pesce contends that the traditional television value chain, which relies on broadcasters aggregating audiences for advertisers, is broken by piracy because viewers no longer need the broadcaster to access content. He proposes a new model called "hyperdistribution," where producers partner directly with advertisers to distribute content globally. In this model, advertising is embedded directly into the video stream as a subtle "bug" or logo, rather than in separate commercial breaks. This approach allows producers to monetize content through branding deals that can cover production costs, bypassing the high fees charged by broadcast networks. He uses the example of Desperate Housewives to illustrate the high profit margins of current broadcast models and suggests that direct advertiser partnerships could be more cost-effective for both parties. The speaker addresses several objections, including the sustainability of DVD sales and the difficulty of discovering content in a decentralized environment. He argues that word-of-mouth and social networks will replace broadcast schedules as the primary means of discovery, similar to how music discovery has evolved. He also predicts that live programming, such as sports and news, will remain the domain of traditional broadcasters, while pre-produced content will shift to hyperdistribution. Pesce concludes by urging Australian producers to adapt to this new reality, emphasizing that Australia’s ability to produce high-quality, low-cost television positions it well to lead in this emerging global market. He predicts that a successful hyper-distributed television program will appear before the end of the year.
