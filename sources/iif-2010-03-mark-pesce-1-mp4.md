# iif-2010-03-Mark_Pesce (1).mp4

## Summary

This recording is a talk by Mark Pesce, delivered to an audience that includes a reference to a preceding speaker named Genevieve. The presentation, set in 2010, explores the impact of hypertext on reading habits, the evolution of the electronic book, and the future of narrative. Pesce argues that while five billion people are now continuously connected, networks are foundational to human thought, a concept he traces back to Ted Nelson’s 1982 book Literary Machines and the subsequent development of the World Wide Web by Tim Berners-Lee. Pesce introduces the concept of a centrifugal force in hypertext, where links pull readers away from linear content. He contrasts commercial news sites, which minimize links to retain attention for advertising revenue, with Wikipedia, which embraces connectivity. This tension has altered news consumption, leading to fragmented reading patterns and the rise of the TL;DR phenomenon, where readers avoid long-form content due to the high opportunity cost of their time. Pesce distinguishes between publishing in light, which is a simple digital facsimile of print, and the true electronic book, which should leverage the medium’s inherent nonlinearity and interconnectivity. He predicts that over the next decade, texts will fragment into dense, interconnected nodes, dissolving the traditional boundaries of the book into a unified mass of human expression. Regarding literature, Pesce suggests that while standard page-turners may struggle against the distractions of the web, other forms will thrive. He cites J.R.R. Tolkien’s Lord of the Rings as an example of a narrative that creates a universe readers inhabit, naturally lending itself to hypertextual exploration. He also highlights James Joyce’s Finnegan’s Wake as a precursor to this future, noting its dense, layered meaning that anticipates the exponential multiplication of meaning in a fully hypertextual world. Pesce concludes that the book is not dying but transitioning into a new form where all texts are interconnected, a shift he compares to the invention of movable type.
