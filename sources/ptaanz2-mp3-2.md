# PTAANZ2.mp3

## Summary

This recording is a keynote address delivered by Mark Pesce, a futurist and podcaster from the University of Sydney, at a seminar for emerging public transport leaders. The event, identified by the hashtag #membertheleaders22, focused on the future of public transport and included sessions on sustainability, accessibility, and career development. Pesce, who lives in Chippendale and relies entirely on public transport, frames his talk around the transition from an industrial to a post-industrial economy. He argues that the pandemic accelerated a shift that had been building since the 1970s, moving the locus of economic activity from the office back to the home. This fundamental change has disrupted the traditional model of public transport, which was designed to move large numbers of workers to centralized factories and offices at specific times. Pesce presents data indicating that while private vehicle usage into city centers has increased, the overall weekday population of Sydney’s CBD has dropped to 52% of pre-pandemic levels, whereas weekend populations have exceeded pre-pandemic numbers. He suggests that CBDs are transforming into leisure and social destinations rather than purely industrial hubs, requiring public transport to adapt to new patterns of movement that are less synchronized and more frequent throughout the day. He also discusses the changing nature of work, noting that while remote work is efficient for individual tasks, it stifles group creativity, leading companies to value in-person interaction for relational and problem-solving purposes. Consequently, the home is becoming a profit center rather than just a cost center, altering material and service flows. Regarding technology, Pesce issues a significant caveat regarding autonomous vehicles. He retracts earlier hype, citing insights from robotics expert Dr. Ken Goldberg, to explain that fully autonomous cars for general road use are likely decades away due to the complexity of real-world variables. Instead, he advocates for a focus on micro-mobility and on-demand transport solutions that can handle varied, hybrid needs. He concludes by urging emerging leaders to abandon industrial-era assumptions and embrace the post-industrial reality, viewing the current disruption as an opportunity to redesign transport systems for a society where work and life are increasingly integrated and decentralized.
