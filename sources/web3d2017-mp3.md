# Web3D2017.mp3

## Summary

This recording is a keynote address delivered by Mark Pesce, a media and technology commentator and former engineer, likely at a conference in Australia given his references to local events and the Sydney Opera House. Pesce traces the history of computing from its philosophical roots to the emergence of the World Wide Web and virtual reality. He highlights the 1968 Fall Joint Computer Conference as a pivotal moment when Douglas Engelbart demonstrated the "Mother of All Demos," introducing the mouse and hypertext, while Ivan Sutherland presented early head-mounted displays. Pesce argues that these innovations were initially too complex for widespread adoption but laid the groundwork for the web, which he describes as a "good idea" that succeeded where perfect systems failed. Pesce details his personal involvement in the development of VRML, the first 3D standard for the web, which he co-created with Tony Bogdanoff. He explains how the technology evolved from expensive military simulations to consumer platforms like the PlayStation, and eventually to mobile devices. A key anecdote involves Pesce testing a WebVR application on a modern Android television, demonstrating that the hardware for immersive experiences is already ubiquitous. He connects this technological progression to the release of Pokémon Go, which he views as a proof of concept for the need to integrate virtual and physical spaces. The core of the talk focuses on the "Mixed Reality Service" (MRS), a protocol Pesce is developing with the W3C. MRS binds spatial coordinates to URIs, allowing devices to query their physical environment for permissions and information, similar to how DNS binds names to IP addresses. Pesce outlines use cases for autonomous drones, agriculture, and location-based services, emphasizing the critical need for permissioning systems to prevent malicious actors from exploiting augmented reality. He concludes with a philosophical warning about the fusion of perception and knowledge, arguing that as virtual realities become indistinguishable from the real world, society faces a crisis of truth and propaganda that requires careful technological governance.
