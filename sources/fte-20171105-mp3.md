# fte_20171105.mp3

## Summary

This recording is an episode of the radio program Future Tense, hosted by Anthony Fennell, which explores the intersection of technology, disability, and inclusive design. The program features interviews with several experts and innovators, beginning with Yuta Tervaranus, an inclusion design expert at OCAD University in Toronto. Tervaranus discusses the "curb cut effect," explaining how innovations originally designed for people with disabilities, such as curb cuts, ultimately benefit the general population by making designs more adaptive and generous. The conversation shifts to the role of artificial intelligence, noting that while AI can improve access for those with disabilities, it often excludes outliers from its data sets, leading to biased outcomes in areas like hiring, lending, and self-driving car navigation. Tervaranus describes an initiative to challenge major tech companies with "secret tests" based on edge scenarios to improve their AI systems. The program then moves to Melbourne to cover the Tom Make-a-thon, a 72-hour event at Swinburne University where multidisciplinary teams design technological solutions for specific disability needs. Project manager Kylie Appel highlights past projects, such as a portable ramp for a wheelchair user and a magnetic bracelet for a person with an acquired brain injury, emphasizing that all created technology is uploaded to an open-source platform. The segment also features British game designer Ian Hamilton, who explains that games are inherently exclusionary but can be optimized for accessibility. Hamilton details a set of guidelines he developed five years prior, noting a significant cultural shift in the gaming industry and community toward embracing accessibility features, which ultimately improves game design for all players. The final segment introduces the AV1 telepresence robot, developed by the Norwegian organization No Isolation. CEO Karen Dalva explains that the device allows chronically ill children to maintain a presence in their classrooms without being physically present. Unlike traditional video conferencing, the AV1 lacks a screen to display the remote child's face, respecting their desire not to be on display while still allowing classmates to interact with the robot. Dalva shares anecdotes about how the device helps children maintain social connections during long medical treatments, such as a bone marrow transplant, making the eventual return to school less daunting.
