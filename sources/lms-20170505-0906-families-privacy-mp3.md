# lms_20170505_0906 families privacy.mp3

## Summary

This recording is a segment from the ABC Radio National program Life Matters, featuring a discussion on the impact of location tracking technology on family dynamics and personal privacy. The hosts interview Joanne Orlando, a researcher in technology and learning at the University of Western Sydney, and Mark Pesci, a futurist and honorary associate in digital cultures at the University of Sydney. The segment begins with a listener story from Larissa Dan in Canberra, who expresses horror at a school policy allowing parents to monitor their children’s electronic classrooms and emails without the children’s consent. Joanne Orlando reports that while many parents feel pressure to use surveillance apps to be considered "good parents," only about 20 percent are fully on board. Children in her research overwhelmingly oppose tracking, viewing it as a violation of trust. Mark Pesci explains the technical mechanisms, noting that smartphones use GPS and mobile networks to provide precise location data. He highlights that Android phones often send location data to Google by default, while Apple offers more granular controls, and advises listeners to review their privacy settings. The discussion emphasizes that tracking is often used for family organization and safety, such as coordinating dinner times or locating family members at events, but Pesci argues that continuous tracking can amplify parental anxiety rather than alleviate it. The conversation shifts to the nuances of consent and the binary nature of tracking. Pesci suggests that temporary, agreed-upon tracking for specific events is more effective than constant surveillance. Listener emails illustrate the tension between safety needs and the desire for privacy, including a woman in her late 60s who rejected tracking to maintain her private life. The panel also addresses the risks of domestic violence, where tracking technology is used to stalk former partners, and the long-term developmental impact on children. Orlando warns that constant monitoring prevents teenagers from developing independence and may lead them to impose similar restrictive expectations on their future partners. The segment concludes with advice to have age-appropriate conversations with children about digital boundaries as they grow older.
