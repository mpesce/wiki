# UQ.wav

## Summary

This recording is a lecture delivered by Mark Pesce, a computer scientist and futurist, at the University of Queensland to mark the 50th anniversary of its computer science program. Introduced by a host who noted Pesce’s background as a co-inventor of VRML and a panelist on ABC’s New Inventors, the talk is titled "A Testing Century." Pesce frames the presentation as a historical review of the origins of modern computing, a look at the current state of technology, and a projection of the next fifty years. Pesce begins by highlighting three often-forgotten pioneers who defined the landscape of 1968. He discusses Jason Reichert, whose "Cybernetic Serendipity" exhibition reframed computers as creative tools rather than mere calculating machines. He then covers Ivan Sutherland, who created the first interactive drawing program, Sketchpad, and the first virtual reality headset, the "Sword of Damocles," establishing the foundation for human-computer interaction. Finally, Pesce details Douglas Engelbart’s "Mother of All Demos," which introduced the mouse, copy-and-paste, video conferencing, and hypertext, effectively demonstrating the core concepts of the modern web. The lecture then transitions to the present day, noting that Moore’s Law has hit a physical wall due to quantum limits in semiconductor fabrication. Pesce explains that this stagnation is giving rise to quantum computing, a field that poses fundamental questions about reality and threatens current cryptographic infrastructure. Looking forward to the 100th anniversary of the program, Pesce argues that the future of artificial intelligence is not about machines replacing humans, as depicted in science fiction like 2001: A Space Odyssey. Instead, he points to the game of Go, where the human player Lee Sedol used the machine AlphaGo to improve his own skills. Pesce concludes that the future lies in "pair play," where human and machine intelligence are complementary, forming a new type of partnership that enhances both parties rather than rendering one obsolete.
