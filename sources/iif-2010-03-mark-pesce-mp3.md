# iif-2010-03-Mark_Pesce.mp3

## Summary

This recording is a lecture delivered by Mark Pesce, a media and technology commentator and inventor of VRML, who is introduced by a host as a futurist and author. The talk, likely delivered around 2010 given the references to current events and technology, explores the impact of hypertext on reading habits, the evolution of the electronic book, and the future of narrative. Pesce argues that the web has fundamentally altered how we consume information, creating a "centrifugal force" that pulls readers away from linear texts toward linked content. Pesce contrasts commercial news sites, which minimize links to retain user attention for advertising revenue, with Wikipedia, which embraces the medium’s connectivity. He notes that this tension has led to the "TL;DR" phenomenon, where readers avoid long-form content due to the opportunity cost of commitment in a hyper-competitive attention economy. He distinguishes between "publishing in light," which is merely a digital facsimile of print, and true electronic books that leverage the unique capabilities of the medium. He predicts that as e-books evolve, they will become fluid, non-linear hybrids that dissolve traditional boundaries between texts, a process he compares to the Schwarzschild radius of a black hole. In the final section, Pesce examines how narrative fiction will survive these changes. He suggests that while standard page-turners may struggle against digital distractions, works that create immersive universes, such as J.R.R. Tolkien’s Lord of the Rings, will thrive by encouraging readers to inhabit the world. He also highlights James Joyce’s Finnegan’s Wake as a precursor to hypertext fiction, arguing that its dense, multi-layered language anticipates the future of electronic texts where meaning multiplies exponentially through interconnection. The lecture concludes with the idea that the discrete book will eventually dissolve into a unified, hyper-connected mass of human expression.
