# hypercasting.wmv

## Summary

This recording is a talk titled "Hypercasting: Part One, The Story So Far," delivered by Mark Pesce. The presentation outlines the seismic shifts in media distribution driven by the internet, arguing that the collapse of traditional barriers has fundamentally altered the economics of content creation and consumption. Pesce begins by reflecting on the evolution of the web from the dot-com bubble era to the rise of peer-to-peer technologies, noting that the digital artifact's inherent ability to be copied has undermined established media ownership structures. He highlights the simultaneous purchase of YouTube by Google and the subsequent licensing deals with major music publishers as evidence that big media is adapting to these new realities rather than resisting them. A central theme of the talk is the concept of salience, which Pesce defines as the primary quality that determines whether information is shared and consumed. He argues that amateur productions with high salience, such as the viral video of the "Free Hugs" man by the band Sick Puppies, can outperform professional content with lower relevance. This shift empowers audiences to act as their own broadcasters, selecting content that resonates personally rather than relying on traditional gatekeepers. Pesce dismisses the notion that audiences prefer only professional media, citing successful amateur projects like Red vs. Blue and Homestar Runner as proof that viewers seek out content that speaks directly to them. In the final section, Pesce introduces the concept of hypercasting as the future state of media, driven by the analysis of social interaction data. He compares the current abundance of unrecorded user data to the early discovery of petroleum, suggesting that these "data shadows" hold immense value for filtering information. By modeling social networks and tracking the reasons behind content sharing, technology can automate the curation of media, ensuring that users are presented with relevant content from their social circles. Pesce concludes that while platforms like YouTube may face challenges, the audience's demand for personalized, high-salience content is the true force driving the transition toward hypercasting, where media distribution becomes instantaneous and deeply integrated into social life.
