# Embiggen-17Aug12.mp4

## Summary

This recording captures a book launch event in Melbourne featuring Mark Pesce, who is introduced by host Adam as the author of The Next Billion Seconds. Pesce, who notes he was expelled from MIT, describes the book as an exploration of the next billion seconds, a timeframe that extends to approximately 2045 and aligns with predictions of the technological singularity. He reads excerpts from three chapters, beginning with a narrative about a woman seeking a neural implant, which Pesce uses to argue that most people already possess effective implants in the form of their mobile phones. He posits that hyperconnectivity has eliminated solitude, creating a dependence on constant connection that leaves individuals helpless when signal is lost. Pesce then discusses the social dynamics of hyperconnectivity, using the example of Melbourne ticket inspectors to illustrate how shared information creates situational awareness. He argues that this networked sharing acts as a force that exposes state surveillance and censorship, transforming Orwellian surveillance states into scenarios where the public monitors power structures. He cites the Arab Spring and WikiLeaks as examples of how hyperconnected populations can route around state control, asserting that the human network persists even when technology is shut down. In the final section, Pesce outlines a progression from connectivity to sharing, learning, and finally doing. He suggests that humanity has moved from Dunbar’s number of 150 connections to a global network of billions, creating a new species, Homo nexus, capable of leveraging the collective intelligence of five billion people. During the Q&A, Pesce addresses concerns about signal-to-noise ratios, noting that human connections act as filters. He also tackles the paradox of confirmation bias in connected culture, acknowledging that while connectivity allows for the reinforcement of false beliefs, it also increases transparency and makes disinformation harder to sustain. The session concludes with a discussion on the trade-offs between digital connectivity and the value of disconnection and silence.
