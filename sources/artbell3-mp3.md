## Summary

This recording is an interview segment from the radio program Coast to Coast AM, hosted by Art Bell. The guest is Mark Pesce, identified as an internationally recognized figure who brought virtual reality to the World Wide Web. The discussion begins with brief news items regarding a suspected human case of foot and mouth disease in Cumbria, England, and a significant drop in the NASDAQ stock market. The conversation then shifts to nanotechnology, where Pesce describes potential medical applications such as nanosurgeons that can identify and destroy cancerous cells. He argues for broad public discussion and government oversight, citing a recent report from the Clinton administration that emphasizes educating scientists on the ethical dimensions of the technology. Pesce outlines the progression from the computer and biotechnology revolutions to the impending nanotechnology revolution, noting that military funding from DARPA is already driving research at institutions like MIT and UCLA. He details military applications, including nanobots that can disable explosives or transform inert materials into explosives, while expressing a personal preference for focusing on positive applications like disease eradication and food conversion. The latter portion of the interview focuses on the convergence of computer graphics and reality. Pesce notes that current video game consoles like the PlayStation 2 and Xbox have reached a level of graphical fidelity that blurs the line between computer-generated images and live video. He predicts that by 2005, the PlayStation 3 will be one thousand times faster than its predecessor, making the distinction between real and synthetic imagery imperceptible to the human eye. The discussion explores the concept of the Star Trek holodeck, with Pesce explaining that head-mounted displays are unsuitable for long-term use due to risks of binocular dysphoria and potential permanent brain changes in children. He references a cancelled Sega project and suggests that future immersive environments may rely on holographic projection or, more likely, nanotechnology to physically create objects rather than projecting light. The segment concludes with a philosophical musing on whether humans will prefer these created realities over the physical world, comparing the phenomenon to existing cyberspace communities.
