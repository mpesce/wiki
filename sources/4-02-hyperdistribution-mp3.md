# 4-02 Hyperdistribution.mp3

## Summary

This recording is a lecture delivered by Mark Pesce, a media and technology commentator and former Apple and Mozilla engineer, to an audience in Australia. Pesce, who has taught at the National Film School, discusses the evolution of digital media distribution from the mid-1990s to the early 2000s. He begins by describing the limitations of early netcasting, where popular content could overwhelm small web servers, and contrasts this with the rise of Napster in 1999. He argues that Napster introduced the concept of super distribution, fundamentally changing how users thought about broadband and media, even though it was eventually shut down by copyright lawsuits. Pesce explains that this pressure forced file-sharing networks to evolve into more invisible peer-to-peer systems, leading to the development of BitTorrent in 2002. Pesce characterizes BitTorrent as a revolutionary form of hyper distribution because it allows a single person to distribute content to a global audience at no cost, with efficiency increasing as more users join. He contends that this shifts the power dynamic by removing the traditional gatekeepers of distribution, such as television networks and movie studios, who previously controlled access to audiences. To illustrate this shift, Pesce cites three success stories: the machinima series Red vs. Blue, the Flash animation Homestar Runner, and the violent cartoon Happy Tree Friends. These examples demonstrate how creators can build sustainable businesses through merchandise and direct audience engagement rather than relying on traditional broadcast deals. The speaker concludes by outlining the "law of hyper distribution," which posits that the more something is shared, the more valuable it becomes. He warns that the era of mass media is transitioning into one dominated by micro audiences, where relevance to a specific group trumps high production values. Pesce predicts that professional content will be overwhelmed by a tsunami of amateur content, as individuals gain the same access to audiences that distributors once held. He ends with the example of a community theater group creating a satirical show called Cherub, which gained attention through a Wired article and iTunes podcasting, symbolizing the new landscape where anyone can reach an audience without traditional industry backing.
