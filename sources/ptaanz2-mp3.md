# PTAANZ2.mp3

## Summary

This recording is a keynote address delivered by Mark Pesce, a futurist and podcaster from the University of Sydney, at a seminar for emerging leaders in public transport. The event, identified by the hashtag #membertheleaders22, focused on the future of public transport and was structured into three sessions: sustainability, safer and more accessible journeys, and career leadership. Pesce opened by acknowledging the traditional owners of the land and noting his personal reliance on public transport, having never owned a car in his nineteen years in Sydney. The main topic of the talk is the fundamental shift from an industrial to a post-industrial economic model and its impact on urban infrastructure. Pesce argues that the First World War industrialized Australia, moving economic activity from the home to the factory and office, which necessitated the development of modern public transport systems designed for synchronized peak-hour commuting. He posits that the pandemic effectively ended this industrial era by proving that work can be performed remotely, returning the home as the primary locus of economic activity. Citing data from Transurban and Sydney CBD population statistics, he highlights that while private vehicle usage has increased, the overall number of people commuting to the CBD has dropped significantly, with weekday populations at 52% of pre-pandemic levels. Conversely, weekend populations have exceeded pre-pandemic levels, indicating a shift of the CBD from a workplace to a leisure destination. Pesce concludes by challenging the traditional purpose of public transport, suggesting it must adapt to serve a hybrid workforce and a leisure-focused city rather than just industrial commuters. He warns against binary thinking, emphasizing that the future will be a hybrid of pre-industrial, industrial, and new post-industrial modes. Additionally, he provides a reality check on autonomous vehicles, noting that despite billions in investment, widespread deployment is unlikely in the near future due to the complexity of real-world conditions. He urges emerging leaders to plan for a future defined by decentralized work, micro-mobility, and a reimagined role for public transport in supporting a post-industrial economy where the home is both a relational and transactional space.
