# Piracy_is_Good.mp4

## Summary

This recording is a lecture delivered by Mark Pesce at the Australian Film, Television and Radio School (AFTRS), likely in late 2004, given the references to current events and the date of October 18, 2004, mentioned as a pivotal moment in television history. The talk, titled "Piracy is Good," argues that file-sharing technologies like BitTorrent are not destroying the television industry but rather creating a new economic opportunity for producers. Pesce begins by explaining how the early release of the Battlestar Galactica premiere on UK satellite television led to widespread downloading in the US, which paradoxically boosted the show's ratings when it eventually aired on the Sci-Fi Channel. He posits that piracy acts as a marketing tool, allowing core fans to spread word of high-quality content, thereby increasing overall viewership. Pesce critiques the traditional television value chain, which relies on broadcasters aggregating audiences to sell advertising. He argues that this model is broken by the rise of "hyperdistribution," where content is shared directly with viewers via peer-to-peer networks. To replace the lost revenue from broadcast advertising, he proposes a new model where producers partner directly with advertisers. In this system, brands would sponsor specific episodes or series, embedding subtle advertising "bugs" or logos directly into the video stream rather than using traditional commercial breaks. This approach allows for global distribution without the high costs of territorial licensing, and Pesce suggests it could even support local Australian productions by covering a larger percentage of production costs through direct advertiser deals. The speaker addresses several objections, including the difficulty of downloading content, the loss of DVD sales revenue, and the role of broadcasters. He asserts that broadcasters will survive by focusing on live content such as sports, news, and reality TV, which cannot be easily pirated or delayed. He also highlights the shift in viewing habits, noting that audiences, particularly young males, are moving away from appointment viewing on televisions toward on-demand consumption on mobile devices and laptops. Pesce concludes by urging producers to adapt to this new reality, suggesting that shorter, funnier content is better suited for fragmented viewing moments, and predicting that a successful hyper-distributed television program will emerge before the end of the year. The lecture ends with a Q&A session discussing the technical resilience of BitTorrent against copyright enforcement and the potential for intrusive advertising in a decentralized environment.
