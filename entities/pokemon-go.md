# Pokemon Go

## TL;DR

The 2016 location game that, for Mark Pesce, is the defining case study of AR: proof that writing data into physical space rewrites human behavior.

## Overview

Pokemon Go (ASR variants: Pokemon Go, Pokémon Go) is a Niantic mobile game released in 2016, and in the corpus it is almost entirely a **Pesce case study** rather than an object of its own. His recurring argument, made in talks from mid-2016 through 2021, is that inscribing metadata into physical space fundamentally alters behavior in that space — and the game's Australian launch is his evidence. The canonical incident: the game placed a PokeStop in Peg Patterson Park, a small park in Sydney's Rhodes suburb, and a single stop triggered a feedback loop that drew a crowd of roughly a thousand people at 11 pm, producing noise complaints and police intervention; the corpus's two accounts disagree on the exact night (13 vs 23 July 2016), but both place it in mid-July 2016 ([[sources/outer-space-cyberspace-digital-depth-hevc-mp4]], [[sources/surveillance-status-arin2610-20oct20-mp4]], [[sources/pesce-siggraph-frontiers-the-ar-dilemmas-mp4]]). For Pesce the incident proves that "altering space changes human behavior", a principle he says tech companies ignore — AR is not a device but a human activity, the binding of data to place, with a lineage he traces from cave paintings through John Hanke's Keyhole/Google Earth lineage of metadata-rich maps to the game itself ([[sources/mark-pesce-digital-depth-in-augmented-reality-mp]], [[sources/arin-2610-internet-transformations-the-ar-dilemm-2]], [[sources/a-digital-reformation-wav]]).

He repeats the case in at least fifteen recordings: SIGGRAPH Frontiers ("The AR Dilemmas", 2016), the VR Creative Cluster, Web3D 2017, the Toronto "Last Days of Reality" keynote (where players at sensitive sites such as Auschwitz illustrate AR treating the world as a colony), the 2020 ARIN lectures, the Voices of VR interview, and the 2021 "Internet Transformations" talks ([[sources/vr-creative-cluster-mark-pesce-9-aug16-video2-mp]], [[sources/web3d2017-m4a]], [[sources/last-days-of-reality-toronto-17oct17-mov]], [[sources/voices-of-vr-mark-pesce-20nov20-mp3]], [[sources/internet-transformations-5oct21-mp4]]). In each it plays a different role: proof of behavior-change (the park), the trigger for his Mixed Reality Service permissioning work ("the immediate need for permissioning in AR has finally given purpose to a protocol I developed decades ago" — [[sources/web3d2017-mp3]]), an argument against commercial AR narratives (Facebook's 2017 pivot, Project Aria as an "omnibiological panopticon" — [[sources/voices-of-vr-mark-pesce-20nov20-mp3]]), and even a business-model analogy for housing developers (Pete board talk, alongside IKEA Place) and a template for regional tourism ("A New Broome", AR spectacles layering Kimberley history). The corpus never records Pesce *playing* the game — he is always at a remove, analyzing its spatial writing. The one sympathetic line: he credits Google's Daydream/Cardboard line with showing phones had enough power for real-time 3D, the hardware that made the game possible ([[sources/mark-pesce-digital-depth-in-augmented-reality-mp]]).

## In the Corpus


- First appearance: 2016, immediately after the game's release — the game anchors every major AR talk from the July 2016 lectures through October 2021 ([[sources/outer-space-cyberspace-digital-depth-hevc-mp4]], [[sources/arin-2610-internet-transformations-the-ar-dilemm]]).
- The Rhodes/Peg Patterson Park incident is the stable core; everything else is the argument he builds on it ([[sources/surveillance-status-arin2610-20oct20-mp4]], [[sources/a-digital-reformation-wav]]).
- Later appearances shift tone: by 2017–2018 the game is a permissioning problem (MRS) and a governance problem ("right to write"), not just a crowd problem ([[sources/web3d2017-mp3]], [[sources/skype-interview-march-2019-mp4]], [[sources/peet-m4a]]).

## Debates / Open Questions


- **Who "won" the AR narrative in 2016?** The game is cited in the corpus as the moment AR went mainstream, but Pesce's reading is anti-celebratory — the game's success is a warning about unpermissioned spatial writing. The game itself never appears as a positive subject in any source.
- **Is the park incident the same event across talks?** Date and crowd size drift slightly (13 vs 23 July; "hundreds" vs "a thousand"), suggesting the anecdote was polished for repetition — a useful micro-example of how a case study ossifies in a speaker's corpus.
