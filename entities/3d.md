# 3D

## TL;DR
Across four decades of the corpus, 3D is Pesce's core technical and rhetorical territory: from co-creating VRML, the first 3D standard for the Web, in 1994, through the 2010 stereoscopic 3D-TV scare, to today's augmented-reality 'digital depth' and the persistent argument that the web should render in depth, not just flat pages.

## Overview
The 3D thread begins at the origin of the web's third dimension. Pesce's VRML work — described in [[sources/vrml-interview-mp4]] as a "three-dimensional equivalent of HTML" built by an open community of roughly 2,000 contributors — recurs across his 1990s recordings: Web Earth at the Avatars96 conference used live NASA satellite data to model the planet for climatology and epidemiology [[sources/avatars96-pesce-leonard-dipaola-512kb-mp4]], and he was presenting 3D web interfaces at the first World Wide Web conference by 1994 [[sources/mark-pesce-session-1-wav]]. At that stage 3D meant the promise of the web as a navigable, human-centred space; he argued then that 3D visualization is essential for subjects like landscapes and molecules that cannot be adequately represented in two dimensions.

The theme changes register in the late 2000s. By 2005 he was already pointing to the mobile phone — with its vector-map 3D chips — as an under-used node on multiple networks [[sources/telephone-repair-handbook-december-2005-mp3]], and by 2010 the public conversation had moved from VRML to stereoscopic 3D television. In 2010 appearances on 7:30 [[sources/7-30report-3dtv-3jun10-m4v]] and The 7pm Project [[sources/7pm-project-mov]] he drew directly on his 1990s Sega VR work to warn that stereo 3D "tricks the brain" with mismatched depth cues, citing motion-sickness study results and safety tests around children's developing nervous systems — the same safety concerns that, he notes, had led Sega to cancel its headset. Reality-check segments on JJJ Hack that year and in 2008 puncture the hype, noting that CNN's "hologram" interviews were not true volumetric capture [[sources/jjj-hack-future-3dec08-mp3]].

After the 3D-TV boom faded, 3D re-emerged in the 2010s as the substrate of augmented reality. His talks from 2016 to 2021 — the "Digital Depth in Augmented Reality" lecture [[sources/mark-pesce-digital-depth-in-augmented-reality-mp]], SIGGRAPH Frontiers "The AR Dilemmas" [[sources/pesce-siggraph-frontiers-the-ar-dilemmas-mp4]], and Web3D 2017 [[sources/web3d2017-mp3]] — recast 3D not as spectacle but as the rendering layer for metadata bound to physical space, with Pokémon Go as the mass-market proof that inscribing data into space changes behaviour. He argues the modern phone is already "an immersive platform in waiting" — ubiquitous 3D-capable hardware means the remaining problems are content and agreement, not silicon [[sources/web3d2017-mp3]]. The consistent through-line, from VRML to Decentraland-era virtual worlds [[sources/uptown-radio-recording-1-2021-04-26-t12-31-18am-2]], is that 3D is not a product category but an interface technology: the computer adapting to human spatial cognition rather than the reverse.
