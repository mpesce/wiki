# How does Pesce think work, employment, and careers are changing?

## Answer
Pesce's anchor claim is that automation does not make humans redundant but re-prices what humans are for. In [[sources/unknown-knowns-31oct19-mp3]] he debunks the predictions that MOOCs would replace teachers and that self-driving cars were imminent, arguing that human connection and complex adaptation remain irreplaceable, and that machine intelligence actually raises the value of mentoring — education should shift from standardised workforce preparation toward amplifying those human traits. In [[sources/f6-non-mintables-mp4]] the same logic applies to business: as AI automates processes, trust and relationships are 'non-mintable' assets that institutions backed by human liability alone can supply. [[sources/trench-talk-ep57-mark-pesce-mp3]] generalises it for the trades: full automation of lines like Tesla's Model 3 remains hard because robots lack adaptability, and the future is negotiated through current decisions rather than predicted.

The pandemic, in his telling, did not create the future of work so much as reveal it. In [[sources/the-bell-can-not-be-unrung-mp4]] he recalls the 25 March 2020 lockdowns: the feared collapse never came, and the continuity of work revealed a capacity for transformation that organisations, their people and their technology had possessed all along — 'the bell can not be unrung'. In [[sources/smartsheet-reimagining-the-future-of-work-mp4]] he argues the March 2020 lockdown showed office workers they had been using 'stone knives' where the IT infrastructure of the 2000s had quietly built 'chainsaws', and he describes the workforce fracturing into three cohorts — full-time office (roughly 25%), full-time remote (roughly 25%), and a large hybrid middle.

That disruption feeds his most concrete prediction: the twenty-hour week. In [[sources/three-day-twenty-hour-caring-mp4]] he predicts most Australians will move to a twenty-hour workweek within twenty years — manifesting as three days for some roles — because the pandemic erased the boundary between professional and personal life and let 'life gain the upper hand', elevating a 'care culture' suited to an ageing civilization. On the demand side, [[sources/citi-how-to-thrive-in-the-2020s-16oct19-mp3]] argues that digital currencies such as Libra will unlock commerce for billions of unbanked people and generate transaction volumes that only machine learning can process — paralleling how the spreadsheet created the modern knowledge worker rather than destroying work.

The connective thread is education. In [[sources/wave-of-automation-2aug19-mp3]] he argues 19th- and 20th-century schooling 'educated once' workers for an industrial culture that did in fact deliver middle-class prosperity, and that 21st-century education is reorganising around a different social contract — a net of relationality and mentoring that supports continuous relearning. He passes the same judgment on careers from the other direction in [[sources/looking-back-10-years-mp4]]: the transition will be easier than feared because the capabilities already exist, and the challenge of the decade is simply finding the path forward.
