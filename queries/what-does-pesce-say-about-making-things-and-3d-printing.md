# What does Pesce say about making things and 3D printing?

## Answer
The corpus contains little on 3D printing as a named technology — it appears mainly as news-broadcast ephemera (a promo clip in [[sources/000000001b06c69c-mpg]] about what "this kid makes with a 3d printer"). But there is a sustained, recurring position on *making*: that the tools of fabrication are collapsing onto the same cost curve as everything else, and that education must follow the makers rather than the other way around.

The fullest statement is [[sources/learning-in-the-connected-century-mov]] (2015), a lecture to educators in which Pesce, freshly returned from judging the Young ICT Explorers event in New South Wales, celebrates an 11-year-old who built a Raspberry Pi playground-access display using skills learned from Adafruit tutorials. He names this the maker movement, traces it from Piaget and Papert's constructivism through Logo, Lego Mindstorms and Scratch, and argues that Scratch's shift to a web platform — combined with the $35 Raspberry Pi and cheap tablets — has enabled *hypermimesis*: learning by copying a global community of makers. The free release of Mathematica on the Raspberry Pi is his closing example of the digital divide narrowing. The classroom, he concludes, is no longer a place but an attitude.

His TV career anchors him on the physical side of making: as a judge on *The New Inventors* (ABC, 2007–2009) he evaluated hundreds of Australian hardware inventions, from multi-tool implements to biodegradable garden guides ([[sources/the-new-inventors-20070321195900-mp4]]). The maker thesis also shows up in reverse in his earliest science-fiction caution: in the Art Bell episodes he walks the "grey goo" scenario — self-replicating nanobots consuming the planet in 72 hours — as the dark twin of a world where replication is cheap ([[sources/artbell2-mp3]]). And in his 2010 Sydney Writers' Festival talk he demonstrates the same logic in publishing: an author can now replicate the publisher's entire distribution function, the basis of his "share this book" collaborative project ([[sources/swf2010-pesce-wav]]).

So the corpus answer: 3D printing itself is a footnote, but the thesis — cheap replicable fabrication, self-directed learning, the author as publisher, the maker as the new unit of culture — runs from 2004 to 2015.
