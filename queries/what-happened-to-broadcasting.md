# Why does Pesce argue traditional broadcasting is dying, and what replaces it?

## Answer
Pesce's core argument is that the internet removed the scarcity on which broadcasting depended. In the 2004 talk [[sources/piracyisgood-avi]] he explains that BitTorrent turns every downloader into a distributor, so a file becomes easier to get the more popular it is, and he argues that the traditional television value chain — broadcasters aggregating audiences to sell to advertisers — is broken because viewers no longer need the broadcaster to reach the content. The same lecture, preserved as [[sources/piracy-is-good-mp4]] delivered at AFTRS, shows that piracy often functioned as marketing: the BitTorrent leak of the Battlestar Galactica premiere generated buzz that produced record ratings on the Sci-Fi Channel, and a leaked Doctor Who episode boosted UK ratings rather than harming them.

His replacement model is hyperdistribution: producers partner directly with advertisers and distribute content freely into the peer-to-peer network, with sponsorship embedded in the stream rather than in commercial breaks ([[sources/piracyisgood-avi]], [[sources/piracy-is-good-mp4]]). In [[sources/4-02-hyperdistribution-mp3]] he frames BitTorrent itself as 'hyperdistribution' — one person can now reach a global audience at no cost, removing the gatekeeping role of television networks and movie studios. [[sources/hypercasting-mp3]] extends the story to the platform era: after the dot-com crash, peer-to-peer networks, feeds and torrents reborn the web, and Google's $1.65 billion YouTube acquisition alongside licensing deals with Universal, Sony BMG and CBS signalled that big media had lost control of distribution and had to monetize the network rather than fight it.

The consequences go beyond economics. In [[sources/hyperpeople-mp3]] ('Welcome to the Swarm') he argues decentralized distribution ends centralized media control and enables amateur creators who no longer need to cater to mass-market tastes; [[sources/the-swarm-parts-i-ii-and-iii-mp3]] makes the same point about the elimination of channel scarcity, which forces studio content to compete with the sheer volume of user-generated material. In [[sources/1-02-libraries-in-the-age-of-infinite-media-mp3]] he reframes the real contest as professionals versus amateurs rather than professionals versus pirates, with distribution costs near zero and audiences fragmenting into micro-audiences; he notes broadcasters responding by offering downloads and turning the internet into a global video-on-demand service.

Pesce also observed the replacement from inside the media itself. In [[sources/01-twitter-and-the-media-m4a]] he argues Twitter was already beating newsrooms to the story — the 2008 Sichuan earthquake surfaced on Twitter roughly thirty minutes before major news services — signalling the collapse of the professional monopoly on breaking news. [[sources/abc-14jul20-video-first-mp4]], a talk delivered to ABC staff in 2020, traces the arc from YouTube through livestreaming apps to TikTok and argues social sharing platforms have become social learning systems where collective skill improvement outpaces traditional broadcasters. His own practice mirrors the shift: alongside ABC panel work he ran the podcast [[sources/the-swarm-parts-i-ii-and-iii-mp3]] era of netcasts and later The Next Billion Seconds ([[sources/pesce-best-current-affairs-podcast-mp4]]), producing audio for direct internet distribution rather than relying on a broadcast schedule.
