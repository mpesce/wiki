# How does Pesce think education and teaching are changing?

## Answer
Pesce's consistent position is that education must shift from the industrial, didactic model — designed to train workers once for an industrial culture — toward connected, peer-oriented learning that never ends. His eLearning 2008 keynote supplies the economic logic: share courseware as broadly as possible because "the more something is shared, the more valuable it becomes", and the value of an institution is not the lecture but the lived experience of attending, which "you can't download an experience... Experience has to be lived, and that requires a teacher" — though he cautioned in Q&A that some subjects sit closer to the embodied end of a continuum whose answers we do not yet have [[sources/elearning08-mark-pesce-20081104-mp3]]. In "Learning in the Connected Century" he grounds that shift in constructivism, tracing Piaget and Seymour Papert's learning-by-doing lineage from Logo through Lego Mindstorms to Scratch, and arguing web-based sharing enables "hypermimesis" — students learning by observing and copying one another's work, exemplified by an 11-year-old who taught himself to build an LED display from online tutorials [[sources/learning-in-the-connected-century-mov]].

The social contract of schooling is revising itself, he argues: late-19th and early-20th-century systems educated people once for an industrial culture, whereas 21st-century education reorganises around "a net of relationality, of mentoring" that keeps everyone moving together [[sources/wave-of-automation-2aug19-mp3]]. At Loreto Toorak he made peer mentoring the concrete proposal — introduce it from kindergarten — and argued machines are best at routine tasks while humans handle exceptions, citing Go champion Ke Jie using AlphaGo to improve and advanced chess where human-computer teams beat standalone machines; he predicted that by 2033 the average worker will spend half their time learning and half practising, as already visible at AT&T [[sources/loreto-toorak-15aug18-mp3]]. His hyper-connectivity lens adds the ambivalence: mobile phones are becoming ubiquitous sensors enabling technological "helicopter parenting", a trend he links to Sherry Turkle's warning that constant monitoring hinders children's independence [[sources/thenextbillionsecondsineducation-1sep11-wav]].

AI sharpens rather than changes the thesis. Addressing tertiary educators in 2025 — thirty months after ChatGPT — he cited Intel outsourcing marketing to AI tools, the reported bursting of the computer-science entry-level job bubble, graduate unemployment, and studies of reduced brain engagement when working with ChatGPT, concluding that current tertiary models are no longer fit for purpose and must adapt to the degree a task can be automated [[sources/facing-the-machine-ibsuniversity-27jun25-mp4]]. The through-line across two decades: learning shifts from a finished credential to a continuous, relational practice — mentoring networks, shared courseware, and human judgement alongside machines.
