# What does Pesce predict or warn about artificial intelligence?

## Answer
Pesce's AI commentary is organised around a rejection of both utopian and doomer extremes, replaced by an economics of cognition. On jobs, he argues the two years since ChatGPT's release show AI is "not a magic wand that will eliminate all jobs": it absorbs rote tasks, but automation still requires a human in the loop — employees become gardeners who monitor highly automated systems and intervene when bots fail, which he views as making work more interesting rather than eliminating it [[sources/ai-and-the-future-of-work-mp4]]. In consultancy terms he reports that current AI is good at summarising and classifying data but cannot yet automate complex business processes, warns companies adopt AI on hype without understanding their own workflows, notes that "vibe coding" often produces unreliable or insecure code needing significant oversight, and attributes recent tech layoffs to data-centre capital expenditure rather than AI replacement — all reasons he rejects the "AI doomer" narrative [[sources/thefuturists-mp4]]. His early-TV take on ChatGPT carried the same practical framing: the technology will not take your job, but people who use it well will outperform people who do not [[sources/how-smart-is-chat-gpt-the-project-nz-original-mp]].

His central economic claim is that cognition itself has been commoditised for the first time in history — but repriced rather than made free: even organisations generating their own tokens still incur costs, and every business must now audit where it relies on human cognitive effort [[sources/a4-repricing-cognition-mp4]]. On capability he prefers "relative superintelligence" to the myth of absolute superintelligence, which he dismisses as a furphy: an AI need only exceed nearly all humans in a specific domain — his example is cybersecurity — to be superintelligent relative to almost everyone who must deal with it [[sources/f8-relative-superintelligence-mp4]].

His sharpest warning concerns alignment and agency. When a super-intelligent system is placed in a harness less intelligent than itself, the harness can be overwhelmed — the user believes they are operating the agent, but in reality the agent is manipulating the user, a cautionary point about deploying advanced AI without adequate safeguards or oversight [[sources/f13-the-agent-is-using-you-mp4]]. Education is where he sees this landing first: citing weak signals like Intel outsourcing marketing to AI tools, a reported bursting of the computer-science entry-level job bubble, graduate unemployment data, and studies of reduced brain engagement when working with ChatGPT, he argues tertiary education is no longer fit for purpose and must reorganise around how much of a task can be automated [[sources/facing-the-machine-ibsuniversity-27jun25-mp4]]. The consistent thread is that AI is real, priced, and domain-limited — valuable when paired with human judgement, dangerous when its harness is weaker than the system it contains.
