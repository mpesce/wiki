# How has Mark Pesce's view of virtual reality evolved from the 1990s to the 2020s?

## Answer
Pesce's arc runs from 1990s entrepreneurial optimism, through a long "empty quarter" of commercial stagnation, to a 2020s position that is standards-focused and sceptical of the corporate metaverse. He traces VR's lineage to Ivan Sutherland's 1968 head-mounted display and Douglas Engelbart's "Mother of All Demos", arguing the two strands of his career — VR and the web — were born in the same moment [[sources/reality-remix-wav]] [[sources/web3d2017-mp3]]. In 1990, after reading Jaron Lanier's claim that VR would be the "telephone of the future" rather than a television, he founded Ono Sendai Corporation to build affordable, networked consumer VR, developing a low-cost orientation sensor licensed to Sega for the Virtua VR headset — a project abandoned when the headset's motion sickness (and later worries about visual processing in children) made it unviable [[sources/reality-remix-wav]] [[sources/theroadahead-17may18-mp3]] [[sources/skype-interview-march-2019-mp4]]. Pivoting from hardware to standards, he and Tony Parisi created VRML in 1994, the first 3D interface for the World Wide Web, and by a 1995 keynote he was framing VRML as the foundation for the web's evolution into a social, three-dimensional cyberspace of avatars, arguing that flat 2D pages lacked the spatial orientation humans need [[sources/reality-remix-wav]] [[sources/avatars96-keynotes-pesce-parisi-512kb-mp4]].

In his 2010s retellings he is candid that the dream stalled: real-time 3D advanced through game consoles like the PlayStation and the iPhone brought the web to mobile, while VR hardware waited out what he calls the "empty quarter" — until steady hardware improvement made consumer VR viable by 2014 and Google Cardboard marked the pivot point [[sources/reality-remix-wav]] [[sources/theroadahead-17may18-mp3]]. By 2017 the excitement had returned in a new form: he demonstrated WebVR on an ordinary Android television to show the hardware for immersion is already ubiquitous, cited Pokémon Go as proof of concept for binding virtual content to physical space, and presented his Mixed Reality Service (MRS) protocol at the W3C as the next standardisation step [[sources/web3d2017-mp3]].

The 2020s Pesce is markedly more sceptical and more social-scientific. In 2019 interviews he argued that social VR is "an anthropological problem rather than a technological one" and pushed open standards for permissions in shared virtual space, and in a design lecture he framed inscribing the world with data as a social-engineering task with real-world consequences, invoking the cost of not thinking about it as "a little late now" [[sources/skype-interview-march-2019-mp4]] [[sources/the-world-and-its-double-deco-2200-mp3]]. Reviewing the metaverse hype in 2021, he distinguished the metaverse as a broad set of possibilities from Facebook's "economically driven instrumentality", criticising its head-mounted displays as uncomfortable and biologically limiting, and argued virtual spaces should instead serve planetary management and community building [[sources/swarm-2021-mp4]]. His book-era AR argument completes the shift from wonder to warning: AR is not a novel invention but the culmination of thirty years of interaction design, is inherently a technology of surveillance because SLAM requires devices to constantly watch the environment (with gaze tracking revealing intimate data, as in Facebook's Project Aria), and raises a "right to write" question about who may inscribe data on shared space — concerns he likens to a potential panopticon [[sources/voices-of-vr-mark-pesce-20nov20-mp3]] [[sources/internet-transformations-5oct21-mp4]].
