# What does Pesce say about social media's effect on democracy and public discourse?

## Answer
Pesce's view moves from early enthusiasm about networked empowerment to a hard critique of engagement-driven platforms as engines of polarisation. In the mid-2000s he described Australian professional social networks as shallow but remarkably fast — operating on roughly two degrees of separation and transmitting news, gossip, and opportunities at high speed — and framed Twitter as a Web 2.0 "brain trust" delivering what he called "hyper-empowerment", where connected individuals could summon expertise and influence no single person holds [[sources/the-nuclear-option-mp4]]. During the 2011 WikiLeaks controversy he argued that individuals increasingly verify information through bonds of trust in their social networks rather than through traditional institutions, and warned that without those reinforced trust bonds the digital landscape becomes unstable; he also predicted governments would respond to information warfare by becoming more restrictive and paranoid rather than more open [[sources/the-drum-youtube-m4v]].

By 2017–2018 his assessment had darkened sharply. In his EGA talk he described Facebook's machine-learning curation as a "global cognitive bias amplifier" that confirms existing beliefs to maximise engagement, arguing this erodes the public sphere and creates isolated realities — citing a BuzzFeed analysis of a mother and daughter seeing different versions of the news — and he characterised the whole dynamic as surveillance capitalism in which pervasive data collection and AI deprive users of free will [[sources/the-last-days-of-reality-ega-8dec17-mkv]]. He located the mechanism in design: intermittent rewards borrowed from poker machines, engagement metrics intensified after growth plateaued around 2015–2016, and a prioritisation of time-on-site over the accuracy of information, contributing to misinformation and radicalisation [[sources/josh-szeps-podcast-17aug21-wav]].

In 2020 he extended the argument to elections and democracy directly. Discussing The Social Dilemma, he explained that recommendation AI models individual users' weaknesses rather than their strengths, building personalised feedback loops that pull users down rabbit holes and can radicalise them into conspiracy movements such as QAnon and anti-vaccination communities [[sources/weekdays-28sep20-the-social-dilemma-mp3]]. Analysing Facebook's takedown of the Russian-linked Peace Data network weeks before the 2020 US election, he argued the American political environment was "hungry for slanted information" and acting as an accelerant for disinformation, questioned whether any platform could police two billion users against agile, code-switching networks, and described QAnon as an emerging 21st-century belief system that re-establishes itself faster than platforms can ban it [[sources/mark-pesce-the-world-2sep20-mp4]]. The through-line: the same connected networks he once saw as instruments of hyper-empowerment have, under engagement-optimised algorithms, become systems that fragment the shared reality democratic discourse depends on.
