# What has the attention economy done to journalism, according to Pesce?

## Answer
Pesce's answer develops in three stages: (1) the economics of attention destroy the news industry's moats, (2) journalists must re-differentiate or be replaced by a billion amateur broadcasters, and (3) the platforms themselves become behavioral-modification engines that journalism's audiences are now captive of.

Stage one arrives with hyperdistribution. In [[sources/the-swarm-parts-i-ii-and-iii-mp3]] (2005) he argues that once BitTorrent-scale distribution flattens the channels, "the primary currency of the 21st century has shifted from money to attention," and that blogs and social networks are replacing traditional news media — relevance and community replace centralized control. The news industry's first telltale sign is in [[sources/iif-2010-03-mark-pesce-mp3]] (2010): commercial news sites deliberately *minimize* hyperlinks to hold attention for advertising revenue, while Wikipedia embraces connectivity — a direct behavioral trace of the "hyper-competitive attention economy."

Stage two is the professional question. In [[sources/01-twitter-and-the-media-m4a]] (2008) he asks how professional journalists differentiate themselves from "a potential billion users who are constantly reporting events in their own voices," warning that institutions building on the old model are "building their foundations on sand." Even the old leverage still worked in 2006: BitTorrent leaks of *Doctor Who* generated buzz that converted into record ABC ratings ([[sources/abc-jjj-14mar06-interview-mp3]]). But the new economics invert the relationship: in [[sources/connectingup09-11may09-mp3]] (May 2009) he contrasts the hierarchy ("the tower") with the cloud, where knowledge pools and behaviors replicate by *hypermimesis* — the crowd can now do the copying that newsrooms once did for it. His IsPY 2016 talk shows the endpoint: crowdsourcing the analysis of government document dumps is "a tactic that traditional media could not replicate" ([[sources/mindstates-ispy-mp4]]), and in [[sources/the-nuclear-option-mp4]] a controversy over journalist Andrew Jaspin reaches him almost instantly through live-blogging and social media, distance notwithstanding.

Stage three is the platform. In [[sources/mark-pesce-futurist-the-last-days-of-reality-mp4]] (2017) he details Facebook's shift toward curation (2011) as behavioral modification: the 2015 revelation that Facebook could alter users' moods, and the 2017 *Australian* report on its targeting — a "weaponization of influence" that runs a feedback loop over its own audience. In [[sources/weekdays-28sep20-the-social-dilemma-mp3]] (September 2020) the engagement AI is named: dwell time and emotional reactions feed predictive models tuned to individual weaknesses. And in the 2019 TikTok report ([[sources/ppm-2019-10-21-tik-tok-mp3]]), Pesce appears as the academic voice on how an attention platform's economics and geopolitics are intertwined.

The net position: journalism's distribution moat is gone (2005–2010), its replacement is networked amateurism that the profession must either join or cede ground to (2008–2016), and its audience is now inside an engagement-maximizing machine that the profession did not build (2017–2020).
