# How does Pesce use and develop the idea of trust — in institutions, platforms, and networks?

## Answer
Pesce treats trust as a resource that networks create and erode at the same time. In [[sources/truth-trust-and-story-26jul19-mp3]] he argues that twenty-five years of connectivity produced a paradox: the internet lets people share and learn as never before, yet amplifies misinformation, and in conditions of information oversupply people subconsciously select facts that fit their beliefs, eroding trust in institutions. He frames trust as a scarce, bidirectional resource — and points to NITV as an example of an organization holding enough community trust that it can convert that trust into empowerment for Indigenous audiences.

His critique of platform trust is direct. In [[sources/lms-20100531-0905-mp3]] he argues Facebook had accumulated the largest trove of marketing data in history and 'cannot be trusted' with it, criticising the company for imposing terms unilaterally instead of negotiating with users; the social graph, he says, is an asset extracted from users' minds and placed into the world, and needs protection. The Cambridge Analytica revelations confirmed the warning: in [[sources/rnd-20180319-1825-mp3]] he details how a personality quiz harvested data from over 50 million Facebook profiles by traversing the social graph, feeding voter-profiling in the 2016 US, Brexit and Kenyan elections.

When institutions fail, Pesce's sources shift trust to networks and to design. In [[sources/the-drum-youtube-m4v]] (during the 2011 WikiLeaks controversy) he argues that as people rely on social networks for verification, the bonds of trust between connections become critical — users verify sources through personal networks rather than traditional institutions. In [[sources/hyperpeople-mobile-mp4]] he names the epistemological condition 'balkanization of epistemology', where contradictory worldviews coexist without a common arbiter. On money, [[sources/knew-money-24sep19-mp3]] offers his compression of the problem: in a village, trust and socialisation replace money; at scale, money becomes 'a marker that replaces the trust'.

Late in the corpus he turns trust into a design discipline, with an institutional anchor. In [[sources/f6-non-mintables-mp4]] he argues trust is 'non-mintable' by AI — people will not trust a computer directly, but will trust an institution carrying human liability, making liability the foundation of institutional trust. [[sources/f14-delphic-oracle-mp4]] extends this to systems smarter than their users: we will either demand formal mathematical proofs of their outputs, or fall back to an oracle-like, Delphic mode of trust. And in his agentic-AI work he coins 'verification design' — designing agentic systems so they fail safely, learn, and are continuously audited, since 'you can't just trust the supervisor agent either' ([[sources/f10-verification-design-mp4]]). His blockchain-era talks ([[sources/blockhack16-talk-wav]]) treat distributed ledgers as infrastructure for exactly this problem.
