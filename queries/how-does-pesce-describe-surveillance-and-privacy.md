# How does Pesce describe surveillance, privacy, and the trade-offs we make?

## Answer
Pesce's core critique is of surveillance capitalism: platforms collect pervasive data and use AI to shape behaviour, which he argues deprives users of free will. At the Electronic Games Australia conference he marshalled the evidence — Facebook pitching advertisers the ability to detect teenagers feeling "stressed or defeated," and the 2014 PNAS study in which Facebook manipulated the news feeds of 689,000 users to demonstrate emotional contagion without consent — arguing that machine-learning profiles act as "cognitive bias amplifiers" that curate personalized realities, erode the public sphere, and accelerate polarization, before warning that the model is expanding from social media into physical space via things like Google's sidewalk stations using facial recognition [[sources/ega-mp3]] [[sources/the-last-days-of-reality-ega-8dec17-mkv]]. In his "Last Days of Reality" talks he extended the warning to augmented reality, which he views as a tool for constant surveillance allowing corporations to curate physical reality itself [[sources/the-last-days-of-reality-ega-8dec17-mkv]].

His sharpest structural point is that surveillance is not incidental to AR — it is inherent. For augmented reality to work, devices must constantly map the physical environment with SLAM, making surveillance unavoidable in a way it never was on the web; systems like Facebook's Project Aria add gaze tracking, which reveals intimate preferences. He warns that AR will merge the non-located web with physical space, raising conflicts over who may "write" digital content into a place — a right that carries the same power to harass as to decorate — and he traced the lineage of these dilemmas back to his own VRML work and Sutherland's head-mounted display [[sources/mark-pesce-20nov20-wav]] [[sources/internet-transformations-5oct21-mp4]] [[sources/pesce-siggraph-frontiers-the-ar-dilemmas-mp4]]. Even family life gets the same analysis: on ABC Radio National he explained how smartphones leak location data by default, argued that continuous parental tracking amplifies anxiety rather than safety, and suggested temporary, consent-based tracking as the better trade-off [[sources/lms-20170505-0906-families-privacy-mp3]].

He is not uniformly pessimistic. Hyperconnectivity also inverts surveillance: sharing Melbourne tram-inspector sightings or Hong Kong protesters' "technologies of obfuscation," networked publics can monitor power instead of being monitored — routing around Orwellian state control, as the Arab Spring and WikiLeaks showed [[sources/embiggen-17aug12-mp4]] [[sources/ntn-20191114-1105-should-we-really-trust-big-tec]]. But the pandemic showed the balance sliding the other way: lockdown life mined more personal data than ever for Facebook, TikTok, and Google, adding a final layer to profiles built over a decade — and his health-data commentary asks whether corporations holding the medical records of 50 million Americans, or his own Fitbit biometrics now flowing to Google, should be trusted with such datasets at all [[sources/good-shout-29may20-m4v]] [[sources/ntn-20191114-1105-should-we-really-trust-big-tec]]. Even money is at stake: he notes China's digital yuan is designed with central recording of all transactions, turning currency itself into a surveillance tool [[sources/mark-pesce-interview-9dec20-mp3]].
