# What does Pesce say about crowdfunding and patronage?

## Answer
Pesce teaches crowdfunding from hard experience. In a 2014 workshop he recounted running two campaigns, the second being the "Lite by Morris Cloud" — a Wi-Fi-connected LED device launched on Kickstarter in October 2012 that raised $275,000 but missed its $700,000 goal, so the all-or-nothing platform returned every dollar. His lesson: set a lower, achievable goal to guarantee success and momentum, because backers are fans and the relationship is emotional rather than purely transactional [[sources/crowdfunding-31jul14-wmv]]. He frames crowdfunding not as a shortcut to investment but as rigorous marketing and customer acquisition, with a concrete playbook — five reward tiers from low-cost acknowledgment to high-end aspirational experiences, a 120-day pre-launch period for building relationships, a 30-day campaign to maintain intensity, a choice between Kickstarter's all-or-nothing and Indiegogo's flexible funding, and transparent communication with backers throughout [[sources/crowdfunding-31jul14-wmv]].

The deeper rationale comes from his hyperdistribution theory. Once digital distribution is free, the funding relationship must also run directly from audience to creator: in 2005 he argued Australian commercial networks were unviable partners for independent creators, pointing to the documentary Outfoxed — funded by political organizations and distributed through social networks — as a model of bypassing gatekeepers to reach communities (and revenue) directly [[sources/forum-just-shoot-it-part1-2005-mp3]]. His "Welcome to the Swarm" argument supplies the mechanism: peer-to-peer distribution ends centralized media control and lets amateur creators produce meaningful work without mass-market catering [[sources/hyperpeople-mp3]], while the "Doctor Who Effect" showed distribution control moving from broadcasters to users, creating a direct economic relationship between producers and audiences [[sources/the-human-use-of-human-networks-1-mp3]] and audiences becoming creators themselves [[sources/abc-jjj-14mar06-interview-mp3]]. In Not Quite Art he celebrated the same shift culturally: digital tools turned culture from passive consumption into active production and sharing, with remix artists as "cultural archaeologists" [[sources/notquiteart-2008-ep02-mp4]].

He is equally clear about the threat to this patronage economy: platform dependence. On the Josh Szeps podcast he described creators whose livelihoods live at the mercy of opaque algorithm changes, users funneled into echo chambers, and engagement mechanics borrowed from poker machines — arguing for regulatory intervention because algorithms are tuned for corporate profit rather than public benefit [[sources/josh-szeps-podcast-17aug21-wav]]. Marketplace alternatives like Quantiacs, where algorithm creators earn a share of the profits their work generates for investors, show the direct-creator-revenue model extending beyond media [[sources/quantiacs-launch-mov]].
