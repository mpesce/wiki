# What is the attention economy, according to Pesce?

## Answer
Pesce's most explicit statement comes in [[sources/the-swarm-parts-i-ii-and-iii-mp3]]: once hyperdistribution eliminates the scarcity of distribution channels, the studios' and broadcasters' monopolies collapse, and 'the primary currency of the 21st century has shifted from money to attention.' Because mass-produced studio content cannot compete with the volume of user-generated material, audiences depend on social networks to filter the noise — attention becomes the thing everyone competes for, and recommendation through one's network becomes the new scarcity.

He applies the same logic to publishing and advertising. In [[sources/iif-2010-03-mark-pesce-mp3]] he describes the web's hyperlinks as a 'centrifugal force' pulling readers away from linear text, contrasts commercial news sites (which minimize links to hold attention for advertising revenue) with Wikipedia (which embraces connectivity), and locates the 'TL;DR' phenomenon in a 'hyper-competitive attention economy' where committing to long-form content carries an opportunity cost. Earlier, in [[sources/abc-jjj-14mar06-interview-mp3]], the leverage of networked attention is already visible: BitTorrent leaks of Doctor Who generated buzz that translated into record broadcast ratings.

The attention economy's sharpest form arrives with the platforms. In [[sources/weekdays-28sep20-the-social-dilemma-mp3]] Pesce explains that social media AIs track dwell time and emotional reactions to build predictive models of users, maximising engagement by serving content that triggers strong reactions — personalised feedback loops tuned to individual weaknesses, which he argues is fundamentally different from traditional media. His own experience shows the human cost: at the [[sources/sydney-writers-festival-3may19-mp3]] Digital Disruption panel he describes stepping away from Twitter after years of heavy use because of its toxicity and the strain of an unfiltered global stream of anger.

Finally, attention is inseparable from Pesce's decentralisation story: in the same Swarm-era talks he treats followers and followees as asymmetrical choices ([[sources/nightlife-twitter-11mar09-mp3]] — you elect whom to receive, 'they don't have to be the same people'), underscoring that attention must be earned and curated by individuals rather than bought as mass reach. Evidence for a single, named 'attention economy' doctrine is otherwise thin across the corpus — the term appears explicitly only in a couple of sources ([[sources/iif-2010-03-mark-pesce-mp3]]) — but the underlying argument about attention as the scarce resource recurs from 2005 onward.
