# What is the metaverse according to Pesce — as VRML's descendant and as 2020s hype?

## Answer
Pesce has a double claim on the question: he co-invented VRML with Tony Parisi, and he has spent the 2020s commenting on the metaverse revival. His origin story is concrete — meeting Parisi in late 1993 in San Francisco, prototyping a 3D interface to the World Wide Web, and presenting it at the first international WWW conference at CERN in May 1994; built on a rendering engine later acquired by Microsoft (becoming Direct3D), the VRML browser put fully realized 3D worlds on ordinary PCs [[sources/mark-pesce-session-1-wav]]. Yet in his talk "A House Built on Sand" he notes that after three decades the metaverse remains largely empty: platforms like Decentraland, The Sandbox, Cryptovoxels, and Meta's Horizon Worlds each claim to be the metaverse, none is interoperable with any other, and by Parisi's own definition there should be only one metaverse — so trading virtual real-estate deeds inside walled gardens is building on sand. His conclusion is that the metaverse is fundamentally a set of human agreements rather than a purely technological achievement [[sources/a-house-built-on-sand-interoperability-in-the-me]].

On the hype cycle, he is a veteran sceptic: the metaverse has cycled through hype and decline repeatedly across his career, resurging now on hardware like the Apple Vision Pro and Meta's initiatives — but current immersive systems still cause motion sickness in most women and men over thirty, because they are designed for young, able-bodied vestibular systems [[sources/vr-simulation-future-of-work-mp4]]. He critiques Facebook/Meta's instrumentality specifically: head-mounted displays are uncomfortable and biologically limiting, and he would rather see virtual spaces serve planetary management and community building than paid-labour environments, while flagging that avatars still fail the uncanny-valley test and that volumetric video-grammetry is the more promising path to embodiment [[sources/swarm-2021-mp4]]. On Decentraland he is blunt: its MANA token is a "Tinkerbell operation" whose value exists through collective belief, its Landrush sales resemble casino dynamics, and decentralization distributes power more evenly than Facebook's model while remaining fundamentally capitalist and speculative [[sources/uptown-radio-recording-1-2021-04-26-t12-31-18am]].

His own definition is qualitative rather than proprietary: the metaverse is not a single place but a set of qualities — interactivity, sensory richness, and planetary-scale connectivity — which is why Twitter or video calls do not qualify. He expects it to arrive as a hybrid of augmented and mixed reality that blends digital data into the physical world rather than replacing it, predicting that within ten to fifteen years the digital and physical will be so intertwined that having only one would feel incomplete; he dismisses the idea that the metaverse is primarily a transactional space and stresses the unsolved problems of representing body language and networking billions of simultaneous users [[sources/leap-interview-13oct22-mp4]]. The practical payoff is simulation: as with spreadsheets, the value of these technologies lies less in immersion than in the ability to simulate — a skill he argues will become core to business when paired with AI [[sources/vr-simulation-future-of-work-mp4]].
