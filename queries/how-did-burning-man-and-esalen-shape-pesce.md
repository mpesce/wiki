# How did Burning Man and Esalen shape Pesce's thinking?

## Answer
Both communities recur in the corpus as *production sites* and *thinking sites* for Pesce: Burning Man as a "temporary autonomous zone" where he built the Speaker's Corner at the Oracle at Arrowhead, and Esalen as the 2001 weekend with Douglas Rushkoff and Erik Davis where his techno-spiritual cosmology was articulated in public.

The Burning Man record runs from 1999 to 2012. In [[sources/1-02-libraries-in-the-age-of-infinite-media-mp3]] (1999) he addresses a gathering of librarians and media professionals at the Oracle at Arrowhead Speaker's Corner, tracing media digitization as a meta-trend. That same year, in [[sources/3-01-the-audio-tour-mp3]], he introduces artist Peggy Nelson, whom he had brought to the event as set designer for a *Christ Superstar* performance — the Speaker's Corner as a curatorial project, not just a podium. By 2009 the format had matured into a recurring three-day series: [[sources/1-01-the-future-of-me-mp3]] opens a Tuesday–Thursday 3–5pm season of talks on "the future of the self, society, and the world." The series survives in the corpus across years: [[sources/2-01-the-future-of-contemporary-art-mp3]] (contemporary art, a Philip K. Dick story hour, the pathetic fallacy), [[sources/2-02-the-future-of-us-mp3]] (the second day of a three-day run, on collective human development), and [[sources/3-05-the-future-of-all-mp3]] (2012), which works through the 2012 Mayan-calendar eschaton — "the third and final talk in a series." Alongside the Future talks the corpus preserves a poetry strand: [[sources/1-02-the-poetry-of-wallace-stevens-mp3]] (an *American Visionary Poetry* course, its third session) and [[sources/2-03-the-pathetic-fallacy-poems-mp3]] (a spoken-word lecture from the Oracular Madness camp, contrasting prehistoric cave art from the Dordogne with the present).

Why Burning Man? His own frame, in [[sources/2-01-the-future-of-contemporary-art-mp3]]: the event is a "temporary autonomous zone" in Hakim Bey's sense — a crucible for radical free expression, distinct from the art world's jargon and exclusivity. The desert is where his "rage for order" and human creativity are read literally, as in the Wallace Stevens sessions ([[sources/1-02-the-poetry-of-wallace-stevens-mp3]]).

Esalen enters in 2001 as a deliberately spiritual counterpart. The weekend is preserved as the *Trilogues for a New Millennium* ([[sources/friday-mp3]], [[sources/saturday-morning-rm]], [[sources/saturday-afternoon-rm]], [[sources/sunday-rm]]) and again under its other title, *Techno-Pagans at the End of History* ([[sources/techno-pagans-at-the-end-of-history-1-esalen-mp3]] and its sister recordings): a multi-day participatory conversation co-hosted by Pesce, Rushkoff and Davis, conceived in the spirit of Terence McKenna's conversations, moving "between long-form conversation and open discussion." The theme — techno-paganism — is the idea that magical and religious impulses persist inside new media and subcultures (the "technosis" argument in the [[sources/friday-mp3]] sessions, with journalist Eric Reiss approaching it anthropologically).

Read together, the two sites give the corpus its oddball cosmology: Burning Man as the secular crucible where his institutions (the Speaker's Corner, the Future series, the poetry course) were built, and Esalen as the place where that secular project was given its spiritual name.
