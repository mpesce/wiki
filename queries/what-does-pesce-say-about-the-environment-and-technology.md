# How does Pesce connect technology with environmental sustainability?

## Answer
Pesce treats the green transition as an engineering and economic problem that must be solved without wrecking the economy — the title of one of his lectures makes the framing explicit [[sources/transition-without-wrecking-economy-mp4]]. He argues sustainability is now a purchasing criterion that travels up the supply chain: in his example, consumers will refuse to buy an electric dishwasher unless it is made with green steel, so businesses must decarbonize not just their products but their materials and processes [[sources/transition-without-wrecking-economy-mp4]]. In a lecture series on the "big three" industrial emitters, he puts steel at roughly six to seven percent of global emissions, locked into a 150-year-old coal-to-coke process whose replacement demands massive infrastructure investment [[sources/green-steel-mp4]]; cement and construction at three to four percent, with the chemistry of setting releasing CO2 and few green solutions available [[sources/green-cement-mp4]]; and agriculture at six to eighteen percent depending on methodology, with ruminant methane a particular problem [[sources/green-agriculture-mp4]].

His most concrete technical argument borrows from Australian inventor Saul Griffith: electrify everything. Replacing diverse fossil-fuel inputs with a single electricity source could cut the total energy requirements of economies like the US and Australia to about 42 percent of current levels, turning the home from an energy consumer into a producer — building on Australia's world-leading rooftop solar adoption — and organizing neighbourhoods into microgrids where homes, batteries, and electric vehicles generate, store, and share power, with design challenges like arbitrating appliance use against solar peaks [[sources/shared-paths-deco-2200-23sep21-mp4]]. In business keynotes he adds that electrification wins on engineering merit, not just environmental virtue: an electric motor has roughly twenty components against two thousand in a combustion engine, and by 2035 bidirectional-charging EVs — a majority of new cars in major markets — will act as massive distributed storage assets at the edge of a decentralized grid [[sources/business-on-the-edge-21apr21-mp4]] [[sources/pesce-business-on-the-edge-essential-19may21-mp4]].

He frames the stakes with numbers from his own lifetime: pre-industrial CO2 of 270 parts per million rose by 49 ppm over the two centuries to his birth, but nearly doubled that addition again within his sixty years, reaching about 417 ppm [[sources/final-thoughts-mp3]]. Yet his long-range talks are deliberately hopeful: in a "2035" address he describes a future where climate change has been solved, tracing the decarbonization of Australia to foundations laid thirty years earlier across domestic, industrial, and global scales [[sources/introduction-and-how-it-happened-mp4]] — the same hopeful-but-engineered sensibility he brought to judging sustainable inventions like the Bio Cube biodiesel unit on The New Inventors [[sources/the-new-inventors-20081112200000-mp4]].
