# How does Pesce define and develop the idea of hyperdistribution?

## Answer
Hyperdistribution is Pesce's term for the condition in which digital content, once released, is copied and spread by the audience itself, wresting distribution from the gatekeepers who owned it in the broadcast era. He traces the lineage from Napster's 1999 "superdistribution" — a massive but temporary, centralised database of personal hard drives, shut down by copyright lawsuits — through the harder-to-trace peer-to-peer networks that followed, to BitTorrent in 2002, which he treats as hyperdistribution proper: a system that lets one person distribute content to a global audience at no cost, and that becomes more efficient as more users join [[sources/4-02-hyperdistribution-mp3]] [[sources/1-02-libraries-in-the-age-of-infinite-media-mp3]]. He had already argued the economic case in his 2004 AFTRS lecture "Piracy is Good": the early BitTorrent release of the Battlestar Galactica premiere boosted rather than cannibalised its US ratings, because piracy functions as marketing driven by core fans' word of mouth; with the broadcast value chain broken, he proposed producers partner directly with advertisers and embed sponsorship into the video stream itself [[sources/piracy-is-good-mp4]].

The idea's companion concept is the swarm. In "Welcome to the Swarm" he argued that BitTorrent lets information "swarm", marking the end of centralised media control and the rise of amateur creators who no longer need to cater to mass-market tastes — with Wikipedia as the parallel "knowledge swarm" aggregating human expertise [[sources/hyperpeople-mp3]]. A Triple J interview gave the numbers behind the thesis: BitTorrent accounted for about 34 percent of all internet traffic, nearly every major film and broadcast was available through it, and Australians downloaded more television than any other demographic; leaked episodes like Doctor Who's generated record ratings on official release, confirming that early digital access can be promotional rather than destructive [[sources/abc-jjj-14mar06-interview-mp3]].

Over time the concept generalises from a media observation into an economic theory of hyper-connectivity. In "Hypercasting" he read the Google–YouTube acquisition and its licensing deals as big media realising it had lost control of distribution and must monetise the network rather than fight it, while amateur productions like Red vs. Blue and Homestar Runner proved audiences do not prefer professional content alone [[sources/hypercasting-mp3]]. In his 2011 "Hypereconomics" lecture — part of The Next Billion Seconds argument that 1995–2026 is an era as significant as the invention of language — he extended the logic beyond content: hyper-connectivity is eliminating market frictions everywhere, from Kerala fishermen using mobile phones to find the best prices for their catch to platforms like Uber, Airbnb and Kogan that aggregate supply and demand, and he predicted these centralised platforms would in turn be disrupted by a decentralised economy [[sources/hypereconomics-flv]]. What began as a way to explain television's future becomes his general law of the connected age: whatever can be shared, will be — and value migrates to the sharers.
