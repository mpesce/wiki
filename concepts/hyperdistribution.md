# Hyperdistribution

## TL;DR

Hyperdistribution is Pesce's signature concept: once content is released, the audience itself carries the copies, and value migrates from controlling distribution to being shared.

## Overview

The concept enters the corpus fully formed in the 2004 AFTRS lecture "Piracy is Good," delivered as the television industry's crisis was becoming undeniable. Pesce's case study was the Battlestar Galactica premiere: the UK broadcast was downloaded en masse by US fans via BitTorrent, and rather than cannibalising the audience, the unauthorised circulation boosted record ratings on the Sci-Fi Channel — piracy acting as marketing driven by core fans' word of mouth. The traditional value chain, in which broadcasters aggregate audiences to sell to advertisers, is broken because viewers no longer need the broadcaster to reach the content; the Australian television oligopoly's high margins, he argued, rested on scarcity that peer-to-peer networks had already abolished [[sources/piracy-is-good-mp4]] [[sources/piracyisgood-avi]]. His proposal was not litigation but adaptation: producers partner directly with advertisers and embed sponsorship into the video stream itself — demonstrated with a Battlestar Galactica clip carrying a retailer's logo — monetising globally without territorial licensing. A recorded audio version extends the argument, predicting broadcast retains live sport and news while pre-produced drama migrates to a fragmented, advertiser-funded model [[sources/piracyisgood-mp3]].

The later definitional lecture traces the technical lineage that made the condition possible: Napster's 1999 "superdistribution" — a vast but centralised database of personal hard drives, shut down by copyright lawsuits — forced file-sharing into invisible peer-to-peer systems, culminating in BitTorrent in 2002, which he treats as hyperdistribution proper: one person can reach a global audience at no cost, and the network grows more efficient as more people join. Its corollary is the "law of hyperdistribution": the more something is shared, the more valuable it becomes [[sources/4-02-hyperdistribution-mp3]]. The idea is inseparable from the swarm: BitTorrent lets information "swarm," ending centralised media control and elevating amateur creators who no longer need to court mass-market tastes [[sources/hyperpeople-mp3]].

Read as a whole, the four sources show a single argument in two registers — one economic, one architectural. "Piracy is Good" answers the producer's question (who pays when distribution is free?); the definitional lecture answers the system's question (what does the network do to gatekeepers?). Both converge on the same inversion of the broadcast era: scarcity of distribution once made broadcasters the necessary middlemen, and hyperdistribution removes the scarcity, so gatekeeping power drains toward whoever can gather and hold an audience. The concept's subsequent career in the corpus — generalising into frictionless-market "hypereconomics" and the attention economy — is the working-out of that first inversion.
