# Virtual reality

## TL;DR

As VRML's co-inventor, Mark Pesce frames virtual reality as a three-decade project of building shared, navigable 3D worlds on the open web — repeatedly hyped, repeatedly reborn through new waves from the 1990s startups to the metaverse.

## Overview

Pesce's VR corpus begins at the industry's founding: he founded Ono Sendai in 1991, the first consumer VR company in the United States, solving orientation tracking by combining automotive sensors and licensing the result to Sega — whose secret 1993 CES demonstration was cancelled months later, collapsing the company ([[sources/vr-creative-cluster-mark-pesce-9-aug-video1-mp4]]). In 1994 he and Tony Parisi created VRML, a 3D equivalent of HTML built by a global community of roughly 2,000 contributors and deliberately kept open and platform-neutral ([[sources/vrml-interview-mp4]]); he credits its success to open-sourcing the code, a lesson that shared resources increase in value ([[sources/lca2011-keynote-mov]]). He anchors the deeper lineage to Ivan Sutherland — Sketchpad in 1963, the 1968 "Ultimate Display" head-mounted display that established head tracking and stereoscopy — and to the 1950s DARPA-funded cockpit work that gave VR its start ([[sources/arin6904-5sep18-mp3]], [[sources/theroadahead-17may18-mp3]]).

A middle wave of recordings tracks VR's cultural meaning rather than its hardware. At Esalen in 2001, Pesce joined Rushkoff and Davis to range across virtual worlds, psychedelics and the end of history ([[sources/techno-pagans-at-the-end-of-history-6-esalen-mp3]]), and on ABC's Encounter he described building sacred spaces in virtual environments ([[sources/rn-encounter-cyberreligion-22aug10-mp3]]). By 2010 he was on Australian television adjudicating 3DTV — a technology he had literally worked on via shutter-type VR — as health warnings met Sony's sales predictions ([[sources/todaytonight-25may10-mov]], [[sources/7-30report-3dtv-3jun10-m4v]]).

The recent recordings return to first principles. Pesce traces the metaverse's intellectual lineage through Vinge's True Names, Gibson's cyberspace and Stephenson's Snow Crash, while distinguishing that broad possibility space from Facebook's narrowly instrumental, economically driven version ([[sources/swarm-2021-mp4]]); he judges the contemporary metaverse "a house built on sand," with virtual real estate trading superficial and lacking interoperability three decades after VRML ([[sources/a-house-built-on-sand-interoperability-in-the-me]]). He treats AR as the older, more consequential sibling — invented before VR with Sutherland's head-mounted display, and demonstrated socially by Pokemon Go's 2016 Sydney gatherings ([[sources/outer-space-cyberspace-digital-depth-hevc-mp4]], [[sources/internet-transformations-5oct21-mp4]]) — and positions VR and simulation as interfaces for future work ([[sources/vr-simulation-future-of-work-mp4]], [[sources/web3d2017-mp3]]).
